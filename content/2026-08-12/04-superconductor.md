---
title: A superconductor thin enough to fold, stable enough to leave out in the air
kicker: Science
author: The Editors
summary: MIT and collaborators grew wafer-scale, single-atom-thick superconducting film that survives open air without oxidizing, a fix that could let quantum computing hardware shrink dramatically.
order: 4
---

Ultrathin superconductors have a frustrating habit: many of the most
promising ones, exposed to open air, start breaking down and losing their
superconducting properties within minutes. That has kept a genuinely
useful class of material largely confined to the lab, wrapped in vacuum
chambers and inert-gas boxes.

Researchers at MIT, working with Lincoln Laboratory, Rice University,
Yale, and Pohang University in South Korea, found a way around it by
changing the order of operations. Instead of growing a superconductor and
then trying to protect it afterward, the team placed a layer of graphene
over a silicon dioxide substrate first, then grew single-atom-thick
niobium diselenide in the sub-nanometer gap underneath — a technique they
call encapsulation epitaxy. The graphene seals the superconductor off from
oxygen as it forms, rather than after the fact. The result is a uniform,
air-stable superconducting film over an inch across, large enough to
qualify as wafer-scale, with high kinetic inductance that makes it
efficient at storing energy in quantum circuits. The findings are
published in *Nature* ([MIT News](https://news.mit.edu/2026/researchers-make-air-stable-ultrathin-superconductors-more-scalable-quantum-devices-0805)).

The payoff is practical: some of today's quantum computing components need
large arrays of junction devices to do what a small sample of this
material could do instead, meaning quantum hardware built around it could
shrink substantially without losing performance. A material that used to
survive only under glass, in other words, may be sturdy enough now to
become a building block.
