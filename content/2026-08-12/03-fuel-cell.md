---
title: A sturdier catalyst could let data centers make their own clean power
kicker: Climate
author: The Editors
summary: Washington University engineers built a nanostructured catalyst support that keeps hydrogen fuel cells stable through 150,000 voltage cycles, a durability fix aimed at letting power-hungry data centers generate electricity on site.
order: 3
---

Hydrogen fuel cells have long faced an awkward trade-off: catalysts fast
enough to be useful tend to degrade quickly, while catalysts stable enough
to last don't perform as well. Engineers led by Gang Wu at Washington
University in St. Louis, working with Brookhaven National Laboratory,
Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, Northeastern University, and the
University of Pittsburgh, built a catalyst support designed to get past
that trade-off entirely.

Their design uses hollow carbon nanospheres threaded with ordered radial
nanochannels that hold ultra-small platinum-cobalt nanoparticles in place,
even as they're synthesized at 1,000°C — hot enough that particles
normally clump together and lose their effectiveness. In testing, the
nanoparticles stayed smaller than 5 nanometers through the high-heat
process, using less than a quarter-milligram of platinum per square
centimeter, and the finished catalyst retained 85% of its performance
after 150,000 voltage cycles, a level of durability well beyond typical
fuel-cell catalysts. The work is published in *Nature Nanotechnology*
([Washington University, "The Source"](https://source.washu.edu/2026/08/platinum-powers-the-future/)).

The motivation is bigger than fuel-cell chemistry on its own. Data centers
could consume as much as 9% of U.S. electricity by 2030, up from roughly
4% in 2023, and much of that demand is straining local grids. As Wu put
it, a data center able to supply its own electricity through a fuel cell —
converting hydrogen directly into power on site — takes that load off the
grid entirely rather than just shifting it around. It's a durability
problem solved in a lab that points, fairly directly, at a capacity
problem showing up everywhere else.
