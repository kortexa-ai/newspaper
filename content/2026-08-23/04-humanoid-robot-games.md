Usain Bolt's 100-meter world record, 9.58 seconds, has stood since 2009.
On August 22, at the opening of the second annual World Humanoid Robot
Games in Beijing, a bipedal robot built by Beijing-based X-Humanoid ran the
distance in 9.39 seconds — and a robot called Lightning, built by the
Chinese smartphone maker Honor, had already clocked 9.32 seconds in a
pre-event trial, reaching a peak speed of 14.5 meters per second. The same
X-Humanoid robot also cleared 2.88 meters in the high jump, well past
Javier Sotomayor's 2.45-meter human record from 1993 and a huge jump from
the 0.95-meter mark set by robots at last year's games
([Associated Press, via WRAL](https://www.wral.com/news/ap/86cb8-chinese-humanoid-robots-smash-human-records-in-100m-sprint-and-high-jump-at-beijing-robot-games/),
[NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/tech-news/chinese-humanoid-robot-lightning-beats-human-100m-world-record-rcna593869)).

The games, held at Beijing's National Speed Skating Oval, a venue built for
the 2022 Winter Olympics, drew more than 2,000 robots from 16 countries for
a five-day program of 51 events, including soccer, table tennis,
weightlifting, and tug of war alongside track and field. A robot beating a
human sprint record says less about human athletes than about how far
humanoid locomotion has come in a single year — last year's robots could
barely clear a meter in the high jump, and this year's cleared nearly
three. "These sports are perfectly normal for humans, but now robots can do
them. I find it amazing," said Li Yanfeng, a Beijing resident who attended
the event. The games are a showcase more than a scientific benchmark, but
the underlying trend they measure — legged robots getting faster, more
stable, and more capable by the year — is the same one steadily making
humanoid robots useful outside the stadium too.
