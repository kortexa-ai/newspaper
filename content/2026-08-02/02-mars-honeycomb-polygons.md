NASA's Curiosity rover has spent nearly fourteen years on Mars, and it
has seen small patches of polygonal ground cracking before — the kind
of geometric texture familiar from dried-out lakebeds on Earth. What
it found while climbing into a valley nicknamed "Valle Grande" this
summer was different in kind, not just degree: a field of honeycomb-like
fractures, each polygon only 4 to 8 centimeters across, tiling the
terrain in every direction the rover's cameras could reach, wrapping
even around the base of a nearby 6-meter butte called "Miraflores"
([NASA/JPL](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-curiosity-mars-rover-discovers-field-of-honeycomb-textures/)).

The panorama that captured it all was stitched from images taken over
two Martian days, June 19 and 20, the mission's 4,930th and 4,931st
sols. "We've seen a lot of fascinating landscapes through Curiosity's
eyes, but this sea of polygons took our breath away," said Ashwin
Vasavada, the mission's project scientist at NASA's Jet Propulsion
Laboratory
([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/07/260731034142.htm)).

Scientists have three working explanations, and distinguishing between
them is exactly the kind of puzzle Curiosity was built to solve: the
cracks could be old mud, drying and contracting the way a dry lakebed
does on Earth; they could come from repeated thermal cycling, hot days
and cold nights slowly fracturing rock over geologic time; or they
could mark where buried sediment was compressed and squeezed of water
as it hardened into stone. Each origin story implies a different
climate history for a planet whose watery past is still being pieced
together crater by crater, valley by valley. The rover's chemistry and
imaging instruments are now working the ridges of the polygons
themselves, looking for the mineral fingerprints that would settle the
question. Whatever answer comes back, it will be a first — nothing
Curiosity has found in fourteen years covers this much ground in one
unbroken pattern.
