title: Physicists link quantum memories across a record 420 kilometers of fiber
kicker: Science
author: The Editors
summary: A team in Hefei entangled two atomic quantum memories over 420 kilometers of ordinary telecom fiber, more than four times the previous record and the clearest sign yet that a city-spanning quantum internet is buildable.
lead: true
order: 1
---

Two clouds of laser-cooled rubidium atoms, 420 kilometers apart and joined by
nothing but a fiber-optic cable, have been coaxed into a single shared
quantum state. The experiment, led by Xi-Yu Luo's group at the University of
Science and Technology of China in Hefei and published this month in
*Physical Review Letters*, more than quadruples the previous distance record
for entangling two quantum memories and marks the first real demonstration
that memory-based quantum networks can outperform sending photons directly
down a fiber over long distances
([Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-08-physicists-entangle-quantum-memories-km.html);
[Physical Review Letters](https://journals.aps.org/prl/abstract/10.1103/ccd6-rf1s);
[ScienceAlert](https://www.sciencealert.com/physicists-shatter-quantum-entanglement-distance-record-with-420-kilometers-of-optical-fiber)).

The setup is elegantly simple to describe and brutally hard to build. Two
memory nodes, nicknamed Alice and Bob, each hold a cloud of rubidium atoms
that can be prompted to emit a single photon whose properties are tied to the
atoms' quantum state. Those photons travel — one from each end — toward a
receiving station in the middle, called Charlie, that watches for a specific
interference pattern. When Charlie sees it, the atoms at Alice and Bob are
confirmed to be entangled, correlated in a way that has no classical
explanation, despite having never interacted and sitting more than a quarter
of a million meters apart. To keep the photons alive over that distance, the
team converted their wavelength into the telecom S-band, around 1,522
nanometers, where fiber loses only about 0.17 decibels of signal per
kilometer, and built a stabilization system to cancel out the temperature
swings and vibrations that would otherwise scramble the delicate quantum
states along the way.

The number that matters most isn't just the 420 kilometers — it's what
happens past 320 kilometers of that distance. Beyond that point, the team's
memory-based approach starts beating the best theoretical performance
possible for entanglement sent directly through fiber with no memory
involved at all. That crossover is the whole argument for building quantum
networks out of memory nodes in the first place: like repeater stations on
an old telegraph line, they let entanglement be relayed in segments rather
than forcing a single photon to survive the entire trip alone, where loss
would otherwise kill the signal almost immediately.

None of this is a working quantum internet yet. The entanglement the team
verified was faint — a concurrence of just 0.046, with real uncertainty
around that number — and turning a laboratory demonstration into a reliable
metropolitan network will take years of engineering. But 420 kilometers is
roughly the distance between two major cities, not just two ends of a
university campus, and that is the real milestone here: entanglement
distribution has crossed from a city-scale trick into something with
intercity reach. Quantum networks built this way promise communication that
is provably secure against eavesdropping and could eventually link distant
quantum computers into a single, more powerful machine. For now, Alice and
Bob just proved they can hold hands across a very long, very ordinary strand
of glass.
