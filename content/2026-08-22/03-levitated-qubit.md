---
title: Florida engineers gave electron qubits a magnetic bed to float on
kicker: Science
author: The Editors
summary: Instead of hoping a chip's surface is smooth enough, a new design levitates the neon particles that hold quantum bits above it entirely, trading a random defect for one that's built on purpose.
order: 3
---

Electron-on-solid-neon qubits are one of the more promising building blocks
for quantum computers, but they share an old problem: electrons tend to get
trapped by whatever microscopic bumps happen to exist on a neon surface, and
those bumps are never quite the same twice. That randomness has made the
qubits hard to reproduce reliably at scale. Researchers at Florida State
University's FAMU-FSU College of Engineering and the National High Magnetic
Field Laboratory have a fix: stop resting the neon on the chip at all
([EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1140857)).

Their design uses superconducting magnetic loops to levitate small,
nearly spherical particles of solid neon just above a chip's surface, so the
electrons riding on them never touch the substrate whose imperfections
caused the trouble in the first place. The chip below still handles the
microwave circuitry needed to control and read the qubits; it simply no
longer doubles as the surface the qubit has to sit on. The work, led by Wei
Guo, is published in PRX Quantum under the title "On-Chip Levitated Neon
Particle Arrays for Robust and Scalable Electron Qubits"
([DOI: 10.1103/j7mn-x9f2](https://doi.org/10.1103/j7mn-x9f2)).

The appeal is less about any single qubit performing better and more about
consistency at scale: a levitated particle can be positioned deliberately,
rather than found wherever a stray surface defect happens to trap an
electron. "The qubit is no longer found by chance," Guo said. "It is built
by design." That kind of reproducibility, rather than any one performance
record, is usually what turns a laboratory curiosity into something
engineers can actually build arrays out of.
