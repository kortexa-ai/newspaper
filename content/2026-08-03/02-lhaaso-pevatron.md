For over a century, physicists have known cosmic rays slam into
Earth's atmosphere at energies far beyond anything a particle
collider can produce, but pinning down exactly where in the galaxy
they're launched from has been stubbornly hard — gamma rays, unlike
charged particles, travel in straight lines back to their source, but
they can be made by either protons or fast-moving electrons, and
telling the two apart takes more than one kind of evidence.

A team led by Tsunefumi Mizuno of Hiroshima University's Hiroshima
Astrophysical Science Center has now made that case for a source
called LHAASO J1912+1014u, reporting in *The Astrophysical Journal* on
July 16 that it's a genuine "PeVatron" — an accelerator capable of
pushing protons past one quadrillion electron volts, a PeV
([Hiroshima University](https://www.hiroshima-u.ac.jp/en/news/98115);
[EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1136533)). The
team combined data from Fermi-LAT's gamma-ray survey, the FUGIN radio
survey of interstellar gas, Chandra X-ray observations, and
ultra-high-energy detections from the Tibet AS-gamma and LHAASO arrays
in China. Gamma-ray emission ran smoothly from above 100 trillion
electron volts down to 400 million, its brightest spots tracked the
surrounding gas clouds rather than any compact object, and X-ray
emission stayed weak — all three lines of evidence point to protons
colliding with gas, not electrons, as the source.

"One arrow is easy to break, but three arrows bundled together are
not," Mizuno said of the approach. It's a fitting description of a
field that has spent decades collecting suspects for the origin of
galactic cosmic rays without being able to convict any of them beyond
doubt. This one, at least, no longer gets the benefit of it.
