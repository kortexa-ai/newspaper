---
title: A crystal's electrons defy the rule that's supposed to shut them down
kicker: Science
author: The Editors
summary: In a topological material chilled near absolute zero, quantum oscillations kept going past the field strength where standard physics says electrons should settle down and stop.
order: 2
---

Push a metal into a strong enough magnetic field and its electrons get
herded into discrete energy rungs called Landau levels; push the field
higher still and you eventually reach the "quantum limit," where all the
electrons pile onto the lowest rung and the oscillations that physicists
use to map a material's electronic structure should simply stop. In
zirconium pentatelluride, they don't.

A team from the University of São Paulo, Los Alamos National Laboratory,
the University of Washington, and the National High Magnetic Field
Laboratory found that electrons in this material — a three-dimensional
topological insulator — kept oscillating well past the point where theory
said they shouldn't, when cooled to 0.7 kelvin and exposed to fields up
to 60 tesla
([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/09/260901010715.htm),
citing *Nature Communications*, DOI:
[10.1038/s41467-026-72885-9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-72885-9)).
The team, led by Julio Larrea Jiménez with first author and doctoral
student Cauê Kaufmann Ribeiro, traced the effect to "reentrant Landau
levels" — energy levels that, instead of climbing steadily away from the
material's relevant energy as the field increases, bend back and cross it
again, and sometimes again after that.

The more interesting finding is why. The researchers ruled out
electron-electron interactions, the usual suspect for this kind of
anomaly, and pinned the behavior on spin-orbit coupling tied to the
material's topology instead — a property of its geometry, not its
crowding. That distinction gives physicists a cleaner way to study how
spin and topology intertwine in these materials, in a system where the
carrier density is low enough to sit close to a genuine topological phase
transition. No application yet, just a textbook rule quietly proven not
to be a rule.
