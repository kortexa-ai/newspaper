---
title: Chemists built a molecular basket to catch a stubborn bond
kicker: Science
author: The Editors
summary: Rice University researchers coaxed the rare-earth metal neodymium into a new kind of bond with oxygen, opening a possible new chapter for lanthanide chemistry.
order: 3
---

Rare-earth metals like neodymium are chemically standoffish: their
electrons are tucked deep inside the atom, out of easy reach, which
makes them notoriously hard to coax into the kinds of bonds that iron
and other transition metals form routinely. A team at Rice University,
led by assistant professor Raúl Hernández Sánchez, found a way around
that by building the chemistry equivalent of a clamp
([ScienceDaily, July 24](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/07/260724061452.htm);
[Rice News](https://news.rice.edu/news/2026/rice-scientists-discover-new-way-metals-bind-oxygen-possibly-starting-new-chapter)).

The researchers designed a "basket" — a scaffold of ligands that holds a
metal atom in a precise position — and placed two of these baskets
facing each other, with six carefully chosen atoms, including a
dioxygen molecule, bridging the gap between two neodymium atoms held
inside. "You can think of it as a basket that allows us to capture
metals and position them in ways to encourage specific types of
bindings," Hernández Sánchez explained. The geometry enabled a type of
bond, called a pi interaction, between neodymium and oxygen that
chemists had long considered unlikely for lanthanide metals, producing
a highly reactive lanthanide-oxo compound.

The immediate result is one bond, in one lab, with one element. But the
team believes the same ligand scaffold should work across most
lanthanides, and possibly actinides too, which would hand chemists a
new toolkit built around metals that are abundant in nature but chemically
awkward to use — potentially yielding alternatives to the iron-based
catalysts that currently do much of the heavy lifting in industrial
chemistry and biological reactions. It's early-stage, curiosity-driven
science rather than a product. But new bonding chemistry for a class of
elements this poorly understood doesn't come along often, and it's
exactly the kind of foundational work that tends to look more important
in hindsight than it does on the day of publication.
