title: Astronomers found the fastest star in the galaxy, and it may weigh a black hole's spin
kicker: Space
author: The Editors
summary: A newly spotted star named S301 loops around the Milky Way's central black hole at 8 percent of the speed of light, closer than anything seen before, and could let physicists measure the black hole's rotation for the first time.
order: 2
---

Astronomers using the European Southern Observatory's Very Large Telescope
Interferometer have identified S301, the fastest star yet found in the
Milky Way, orbiting the supermassive black hole Sagittarius A* at roughly
25,000 kilometers per second — about 8 percent of the speed of light
([ESO](https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso2612/)). The discovery, led by
Felix Mang and Reinhard Genzel's team at the Max Planck Institute for
Extraterrestrial Physics, is published in Nature
([DOI: 10.1038/s41586-026-10894-w](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10894-w)).

S301 is an ordinary, sun-like star on an unusually extreme orbit: it swings
within about 1.78 billion kilometers of the black hole at closest approach —
roughly 12 times the Earth-Sun distance, only a bit farther out than
Saturn — and completes a full loop in just 8.7 years. No star has ever been
tracked that close in. Researchers think it is the surviving half of a
binary system torn apart by the black hole's tides, with its former
companion flung out of the galaxy entirely.

The proximity is the point. A spinning black hole is thought to drag the
fabric of spacetime around it, an effect called frame dragging, and S301's
tight, fast orbit puts it close enough to feel that drag directly. Team
member Felix Mang said the goal now is to measure the spin of Sagittarius
A* within about a decade — which would be the first direct measurement of a
supermassive black hole's rotation, and a genuine test of a prediction
Einstein's equations make but no one has yet been able to check this way.

The team traced S301's orbit back to 2017 using the VLTI's GRAVITY+
instrument, which combines light from four 8-meter telescopes into the
resolving power of a much larger one. They'll get their next good look when
the star swings past again in 2031, with ESO's Extremely Large Telescope
also watching.
