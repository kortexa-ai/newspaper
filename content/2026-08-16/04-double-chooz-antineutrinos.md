---
title: Physicists caught the faint afterglow of a shut-down nuclear reactor for the first time
kicker: Science
author: The Editors
summary: The Double Chooz experiment measured antineutrinos still leaking from a French reactor plant weeks after both units went offline, opening a possible new way to verify that spent fuel is where it's supposed to be.
order: 4
---

Antineutrinos are famously hard to catch — they pass through ordinary
matter, including planets, almost without interacting at all. Physicists
normally hunt for them near operating reactors, where nuclear fission
produces them in bulk. The Double Chooz collaboration, led by Anthony
Onillon and Thierry Lasserre of the Max Planck Institute for Nuclear
Physics in Heidelberg, went looking somewhere stranger: a reactor that had
already been switched off
([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/08/260814011036.htm);
[EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1138527)). The
result, published August 4 in Physical Review Letters, is the first direct
measurement of antineutrinos emitted by spent fuel and residual
radioactivity after shutdown.

Working from a detector holding more than 30 cubic meters of liquid
scintillator, buried some 400 meters from the two reactor cores at the
Chooz power plant in northern France, the team watched for 17.2 days while
both units sat idle. They recorded roughly 100 antineutrino candidate
events — a faint signal, but one that closely matched predictions for the
slow radioactive decay still going on inside the shut-down cores and their
nearby spent-fuel cooling pools.

The appeal isn't just proving physicists can detect an even fainter signal
than before, though that took genuinely careful background-noise work. It's
what the technique could be used for: antineutrino detectors can't be
fooled or blocked the way cameras and seals can, which makes them an
intriguing candidate for verifying, independently, that a reactor is truly
offline and that its spent fuel hasn't quietly gone missing — a capability
with real relevance to nuclear safety inspections and international
non-proliferation safeguards, not just to fundamental physics.
