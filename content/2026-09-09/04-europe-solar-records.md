It's easy to miss how routine record-breaking has become for European solar power, precisely because it keeps happening. In the first week of September, five countries set new national records for solar generation on a single September day, according to analysis by AleaSoft Energy Forecasting reported by pv magazine
([pv magazine](http://www.pv-magazine.com/2026/09/08/european-markets-set-daily-solar-records-for-september/)). Portugal generated 31 GWh on September 2. Spain hit 267 GWh the next day. France and Italy both broke their September records on September 4, at 155 GWh and 143 GWh respectively. Germany capped the week with 389 GWh on September 6.

Those aren't peak-instant numbers, either — they're full-day generation totals, which means the panels installed across those grids are now large enough, collectively, to move a meaningful share of a modern economy's daily electricity on a sunny day, even in September, past the summer solstice and well into the year's second half.

Worth saying plainly: European electricity prices also rose sharply that same week, as heat drove up demand, wind output dropped, and natural gas prices climbed on tightening supply from the Middle East. Solar didn't cause that — it's the reason those prices weren't worse. Every gigawatt-hour on the list above is power that didn't have to come from gas plants exposed to that price spike. That's the case for building more of it, not less: the more of the grid that solar and storage can cover, the smaller the bill when gas gets expensive for reasons that have nothing to do with the sun.
