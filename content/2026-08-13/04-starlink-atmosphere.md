---
title: Scientists turned 1,200 Starlink satellites into a scanner for the sky
kicker: Science
author: The Editors
summary: A Kyoto University team borrowed a trick from medical imaging to map Earth's thermosphere using nothing but public orbital data from satellites launched for a different job entirely.
order: 4
---

Starlink satellites were built to beam internet down, not to study the
atmosphere they fly through. A team led by Mamoru Yamamoto at Kyoto
University's Research Institute for Sustainable Humanosphere found a way
to make them do both, publishing the first tomographic map of the
thermosphere built entirely from public satellite data
([Kyoto University](https://www.kyoto-u.ac.jp/en/research-news/2026-08-04);
[ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/08/260812015212.htm)).

The thermosphere is the thin, electrically active layer of atmosphere
roughly 100 to 1,000 kilometers up, and it is notoriously hard to
observe directly — too high for weather balloons, too low for most
scientific satellites to linger in without their orbits decaying. Every
satellite that does pass through it, though, gets subtly slowed by its
drag, and that drag is a direct readout of how dense the air is at that
point. Yamamoto's team applied tomography, the same reconstruction
technique that turns a series of X-ray slices into a 3D medical scan, to
publicly available orbital-decay data from roughly 1,200 Starlink
satellites orbiting at about 500 kilometers, building out a
latitude-longitude map of thermospheric density rather than just a line
along one satellite's path
([Kyoto University](https://www.kyoto-u.ac.jp/en/research-news/2026-08-04)).

The map lined up closely with independent measurements from the European
Space Agency's SWARM satellites, a check that gives the method real
credibility. "This is a multidisciplinary study between space science and
space engineering," Yamamoto said of the approach, which was published in
the journal *Earth, Planets and Space*
([DOI: 10.1186/s40623-026-02509-5](https://www.kyoto-u.ac.jp/en/research-news/2026-08-04)).

It is a small, unglamorous kind of discovery — no new instrument, no new
launch, just a cleverer read of exhaust data that thousands of commercial
satellites were already producing. As Starlink and its rivals put ever
more hardware in low orbit, that fleet is turning, almost by accident,
into one of the better sensor networks humanity has for its own sky.
