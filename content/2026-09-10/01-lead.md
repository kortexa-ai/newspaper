Only three objects from beyond our solar system have ever been caught passing through it. The first, 'Oumuamua, was gone before anyone could point much more than a handful of telescopes at it. The second, comet 2I/Borisov, lingered a little longer. The third, 3I/ATLAS, discovered in July, has stayed around long enough for astronomers to do something genuinely new with it: read its ion tail closely enough to reconstruct the conditions under which it was born, around a star none of us will ever see.

The work comes from Dr. Léa Ferellec at Northumbria University's School of Engineering, Physics and Mathematics, using a relatively new instrument called WEAVE — the WHT Enhanced Area Velocity Explorer — in its Large Integral Field Unit mode, mounted on the Isaac Newton Group's 4.2-metre William Herschel Telescope in the Canary Islands
([Universe Today](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/new-revelations-about-the-origin-of-interstellar-comet-3iatlas);
[phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-09-interstellar-visitor-3iatlas-clues-frozen.html)).
WEAVE's particular strength is sensitivity across a wide field in the blue end of the optical spectrum, exactly where the ionized gases boiling off a comet's surface give themselves away. Pointed at 3I/ATLAS as it moved outward from the sun, the instrument picked out five distinct ions in the comet's plasma tail simultaneously — enough to work with the kind of chemical ratios that double as a thermometer for a 4-billion-year-old deep freeze.

The key number was the ratio of dinitrogen gas to carbon monoxide. Both freeze out of a forming solar system's gas cloud, but at very different temperatures, and dinitrogen only survives in a comet's ice if it condensed somewhere brutally cold. What Ferellec's team found was a ratio consistent with formation below about -240°C — colder than Pluto, colder than almost anywhere in our own solar system gets. That points to an origin far out in the icy suburbs of another star system, the kind of neighborhood our own Kuiper Belt and Oort Cloud occupy here, rather than anywhere near a stellar furnace.

The findings, published in *Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society*, matter less for the specific number than for what the method demonstrates. "This object gives us a rare chance to study material that formed somewhere completely different from our own Solar System," Ferellec said. Every prior comet chemists have analyzed this way was built from the same original cloud of gas and dust that made the Sun, Earth, and everything on it — useful for understanding our own history, but a sample size of one system. 3I/ATLAS is a genuine outsider, carrying the chemical fingerprint of wherever it actually came from.

"Powerful, large-format IFUs with high sensitivity in the blue optical spectrum — like WEAVE-LIFU on the WHT — are opening new frontiers for the study of comets and other solar system objects," Ferellec noted, and that's the other half of the story: the technique itself is new and repeatable. WEAVE only recently came online, and interstellar objects, once vanishingly rare sightings, are now being caught by wide-field sky surveys with enough regularity that astronomers can reasonably expect a fourth, and a fifth, in the years ahead. Each one will be a chance to run the same test again — a slow, patient census of the chemistry of other solar systems, taken one frozen visitor at a time.
