Ordinary photonic crystals control light by patterning a material in
space — fixed structures that reflect or bend certain wavelengths and
not others. A team from École Polytechnique, Collège de France, and
the Helmholtz-Zentrum Dresden-Rossendorf has now built the first
all-optical version that works in time instead: a material whose
optical properties can be flipped, over and over, on picosecond
timescales, fast enough to approach the oscillation speed of the light
passing through it
([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/07/260731034131.htm)).

The device is a plasmonic metamaterial — tiny gold structures patterned
onto indium antimonide, a semiconductor — driven by an intense
terahertz pulse from HZDR's TELBE facility. That pulse periodically
reshapes how the material reflects light, cutting photon losses in
half compared to earlier attempts at similar control. The work,
published in *Nature*
(DOI: [10.1038/s41586-026-10825-9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10825-9)),
is credited to lead author Tingwen Guo, a PhD student at École
Polytechnique. "By extending photonic crystals from space to time, we
open a new dimension for light control — and a novel path toward
amplification and lasing," Guo said of the result.

The near-term applications are speculative but specific: terahertz
communications for future 6G networks, ultrafast optical switches for
light-based computing, and an entirely new class of tunable
terahertz lasers. It's early, laboratory-bench physics — but it's the
kind of first demonstration that new categories of hardware tend to
be built on.
