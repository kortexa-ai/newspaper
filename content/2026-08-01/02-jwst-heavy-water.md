WASP-39b is not a hospitable place — a gas giant orbiting more than
twenty times closer to its star than Earth orbits the Sun, baked to
nearly 1,000 degrees Celsius. But a planet doesn't have to be livable
to tell scientists something worth knowing, and a new look at James
Webb Space Telescope data has found a genuine first there: semi-heavy
water, a molecule called HDO in which one of water's two hydrogen
atoms is swapped for its heavier isotope, deuterium
([ScienceAlert](https://www.sciencealert.com/jwst-just-found-a-habitability-clue-in-a-distant-planets-water)).

The detection comes from researchers led by Fabian Gruebel, who ran
atmospheric retrieval models against JWST's transmission spectrum of
the planet — light from its host star filtered through its atmosphere
as it passes in front — and found the ratio of deuterium to ordinary
hydrogen in the planet's water was "significantly elevated" compared
to the gas giants in our own solar system
([arXiv:2607.19579](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.19579)).

That ratio matters because it works like a chemical fingerprint. Light
hydrogen escapes a planet's atmosphere more easily than the heavier
deuterium, so a planet that has spent a long time baking near its star
tends to accumulate a higher proportion of heavy water over time.
Alternatively, WASP-39b may simply have formed farther out in its
system, beyond the "snow line" where water ice is abundant, and
carried that heavy-water-rich material inward as it migrated closer to
its star. Distinguishing between those two stories is exactly what a
precise D/H measurement is for.

Either explanation is a small piece of a much larger puzzle: how
planets end up where astronomers find them, and what their chemistry
can reveal about a past that's otherwise unobservable. It's also a
reminder of what JWST was built to do. Detecting an isotope ratio in
the atmosphere of a world 700 light-years away, rather than just its
broad chemical makeup, is the kind of measurement that simply wasn't
possible before this telescope — and it's the same technique
scientists will eventually turn on smaller, cooler, more Earth-like
planets in the search for what makes a world livable.
