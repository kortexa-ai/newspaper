---
title: NASA plans an orbital dress rehearsal for two different Moon landers
kicker: Space
author: The Editors
summary: Before anyone lands on the Moon again, Orion will practice docking with both SpaceX's Starship and Blue Origin's Blue Moon lander in Earth orbit.
order: 2
---

Artemis III was originally billed as NASA's return to the lunar surface.
It has since been repurposed into something arguably more useful for the
program's long game: a full orbital checkout of two independent human
landing systems before either one is trusted with a real landing. NASA
detailed the plan on July 15, and it's an ambitious one — Orion astronauts
will rendezvous and dock separately with prototype landers from both
SpaceX and Blue Origin while still in low Earth orbit
([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/07/260722032113.htm),
corroborated by [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-07-nasa-artemis-iii-lander-pave.html)).

The choreography, once detailed, resembles Apollo 9's shakedown of the
lunar module before anyone flew it to the Moon. Blue Origin's Blue Moon
lander launches first and can loiter in orbit for up to 30 days. SpaceX's
Starship, on its Version 3 hardware, then docks nose-to-nose with Orion.
Blue Moon docks separately, to Orion's side, where up to two astronauts
will cross over, enter the cabin, and — for the first time in this
campaign — practice donning lunar spacesuits inside a real lander rather
than a mockup. The crew stays aboard Orion throughout the Starship test.

"Both companies have put forward a list of aggressive objectives and
goals intended to complement upcoming uncrewed demonstration missions at
the Moon, so that we can gain both understanding and confidence," said
Steve Creech, who runs NASA's Human Landing System Program. Artemis
program manager Jeremy Parsons called it "one of the most complex and
ambitious missions NASA has ever undertaken."

The point of running two landers through the same gauntlet, rather than
picking one, is redundancy: if either company's hardware hits delays or
technical trouble, NASA isn't stuck waiting on a single vendor to land
astronauts on the Moon. The mission flies in 2027; an actual lunar
landing, Artemis IV, is targeted for 2028. It's a slower path to the
surface than the original plan, but a considerably more tested one.
