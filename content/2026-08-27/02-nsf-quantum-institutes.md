---
title: NSF puts $290 million behind eight university quantum research hubs
kicker: Science
author: The Editors
summary: The funding renews five existing Quantum Leap Challenge Institutes and stands up three new ones, spreading work on quantum computing, sensing, and error correction across 36 institutions in 19 states.
order: 2
---

The National Science Foundation announced Tuesday that it is putting more
than $290 million behind eight Quantum Leap Challenge Institutes, each
built around a different piece of the quantum puzzle: fault-tolerant
computing, hybrid architectures, superconducting hardware manufacturing,
error correction, and quantum simulation among them
([NSF](https://www.nsf.gov/news/eight-nsf-research-institutes-propel-us-quantum-science-290m)).
Five of the eight are renewals of institutes NSF first funded in 2020;
three — including a Princeton-led effort on manufacturing quantum
processors and a Colorado-led institute on quantum systems — are new. Each
gets roughly $28 to $37 million spread over five years.

The awards run through NSF's Quantum Leap Challenge Institutes program,
created in 2020 to carry out the 2018 National Quantum Initiative Act. What
that program has actually produced since then is worth noting alongside the
new money: institutes funded in the first round helped develop new
approaches to building quantum computers and quantum sensors, the kind of
foundational hardware work that rarely makes headlines on its own but
underpins everything built on top of it.

The eight institutes are led by Harvard, Yale, Princeton, UC Berkeley, the
University of Chicago, the University of Colorado, the University of
Illinois, and the University of Maryland, but the funding's reach is wider
than that list suggests — NSF says the work spans 36 higher-education
institutions across 19 states, pulling in smaller research universities and
minority-serving institutions as partners rather than concentrating the
money at a handful of elite labs. "For more than four decades, NSF has been
laying foundational groundwork of research powering modern quantum
computing," said Brian Stone, who is performing the duties of NSF director.

Quantum computing has spent years oscillating between overhyped and
underappreciated in the public conversation, but the actual engineering
problems it needs solved — building qubits that hold their state longer,
correcting errors without an unworkable overhead, and manufacturing
hardware that doesn't require a one-of-a-kind lab setup for every device —
are exactly the unglamorous, multi-year problems that steady university
funding is built to chip away at. Five more years of it, spread across
three dozen institutions, is a reasonable bet on getting there.
