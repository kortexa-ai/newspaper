---
title: A cartilage trick could keep EV batteries from cracking up
kicker: Climate
author: The Editors
summary: Hong Kong researchers borrowed a design principle from joint cartilage to coat battery cathodes, cushioning the microcracks that quietly drain lithium-ion batteries over time.
order: 3
---

The cathodes that give lithium-ion batteries their energy density — the
nickel-rich oxides packed into EVs and phones — have a durability problem:
they swell and shrink every time the battery charges, and over hundreds of
cycles that flexing opens microscopic cracks that sap capacity. Researchers
at the Hong Kong Polytechnic University and the Hong Kong University of
Science and Technology have built a coating that addresses the cracking
directly, by borrowing a trick from cartilage, the tissue that lets a joint
absorb repeated impact without shattering
([Tech Xplore](https://techxplore.com/news/2026-08-cartilage-lithium-battery-coating-cushions.html)).

Published in Nature Nanotechnology
([DOI: 10.1038/s41565-026-02224-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41565-026-02224-y)),
the work applies an ultrathin, flexible shape-memory polymer to the grain
boundaries of cathode particles — the seams where cracking usually starts.
Rather than trying to make the material harder and more crack-resistant,
the coating does the opposite: it flexes under stress and springs back,
spreading the mechanical load across a wider area instead of letting it
concentrate at one weak point. "Instead of making the material harder, we
make it softer at targeted points to absorb and dissipate the energy," said
Qiang Liu, a corresponding author on the paper. Tested on both nickel-rich
layered oxides and lithium iron phosphate, coated particles held their
structure noticeably better than uncoated ones over repeated cycling.

It is a small, quiet fix rather than a new battery chemistry, which is part
of the appeal: a coating that can be applied to cathode materials already
in mass production is a far shorter path to real-world batteries than
inventing one from scratch. The team is now testing the approach in
solid-state batteries and working on higher-tolerance versions of the
polymer, with an eye toward the kind of unglamorous durability gain that
adds real miles to an EV's usable life without anyone having to change how
the battery is made.
