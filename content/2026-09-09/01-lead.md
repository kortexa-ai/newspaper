Einstein's equivalence principle is one of those ideas so foundational it rarely gets tested directly anymore: drop anything in a gravitational field, with no other forces acting on it, and it falls the same way regardless of what it's made of. It underwrites all of general relativity. Physicists have checked it with falling apples, falling satellites, and falling atoms for decades. What nobody had managed was watching it apply to a single quantum object in freefall, at the scale where quantum mechanics' rules of probability and interference take over from Newton's tidy lines.

An international team led by Professor Ron Folman at Ben-Gurion University of the Negev, working with the University of Ulm, the University of Oxford, the University of Southampton, the German Aerospace Center, and Texas A&M, has now done exactly that. Their paper, published September 2 in *Science Advances*, reports the first direct measurement of the quantum phase a freely falling object accumulates under gravity — and it lines up with what Einstein's century-old principle predicts
([Oxford Department of Physics](https://www.physics.ox.ac.uk/news/scientists-observe-einsteins-gravity-quantum-world);
[ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/09/260907201552.htm);
[phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-09-scientists-einstein-gravity-quantum-world.html)).

The experiment, which the team calls a Quantum Galileo Interferometer, works by exploiting a strange but well-established quantum trick: a single atom's wave function can be split into two paths and sent on different journeys before being recombined. The researchers cooled rubidium atoms to just above absolute zero on a specialized atom chip, then split each atom's quantum wave in two. One path was held stationary by magnetic fields and microwave pulses; the other was released to fall freely. When the two paths were brought back together, the interference pattern between them revealed exactly how much quantum phase the falling half had picked up during its drop — a number set entirely by gravity's pull, if Einstein is right.

He was. The measured phase matched the prediction from applying the equivalence principle to a quantum system, meaning gravity behaved exactly as consistently for the falling atom's wave as it does for a falling apple, at a scale where quantum uncertainty and interference are the dominant physics.

The result matters because it closes a stubborn gap between two enormously successful but famously uncooperative theories. "This is a unique paper, in the sense that it combines a hard experiment with a far-reaching theoretical interpretation," said Folman. Oxford's Professor Vlatko Vedral, a co-author, put the stakes plainly: "This experiment pushes quantum mechanics into one of its most intriguing frontiers, gravity, and shows that its predictions hold." The team also included Sir Roger Penrose, the Nobel laureate whose own work has spent decades probing exactly this seam between relativity and the quantum world.

Nobody expected Einstein to be wrong here — the equivalence principle has survived every previous test. But every test closes a door that theorists trying to unify gravity with quantum mechanics might otherwise have slipped through, and doing the test at all required building an interferometer precise enough to catch a phase shift induced by nothing but a fall. That kind of precision, once demonstrated, tends to get reused: the same atom-chip techniques are candidates for next-generation gravity sensors and tests of dark energy models that predict tiny deviations from Einstein's predictions at other scales. For now, the story is simpler and, in its way, more satisfying: dropped gently enough and measured carefully enough, even a quantum wave falls the way Galileo said everything should.
