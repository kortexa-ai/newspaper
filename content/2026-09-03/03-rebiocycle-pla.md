---
title: A European project closed the loop on recycling bioplastic — outside the lab
kicker: Climate
author: The Editors
summary: The ReBioCycle consortium recovered PLA from mixed plastic waste at pilot scale and turned it back into plastic as good as new, a step toward genuinely circular packaging.
order: 3
---

PLA — the corn-starch-derived bioplastic used in everything from compostable
cutlery to food packaging — has long had an inconvenient habit for a
material marketed as green: once it's mixed in with other plastics in a
household waste stream, it has been very hard to recover and reuse. This
week, the EU-backed ReBioCycle consortium announced it had cracked that
problem at pilot scale, not just in a lab beaker
([ReBioCycle](https://rebiocycle.eu/); reported by
[Packaging Gateway](https://www.packaging-gateway.com/news/eu-project-pla-recycling-mixed-waste/)).

The process links three separately proven technologies into one chain.
First, PLA is sorted out of mixed plastic waste using existing sorting
infrastructure — the announcement's most practical detail, since it means
no new collection systems are required. Next, a hydrolysis process
developed by the Dutch company Torwash breaks the sorted PLA down into
lactic acid, its primary chemical building block. Finally, TotalEnergies
Corbion — the joint venture that already produces PLA commercially —
repolymerizes that lactic acid into new PLA with properties the consortium
says match virgin material.

"This is about making circularity real for industry," said project
coordinator Kevin O'Connor. "We've connected the full value chain in a way
that supports both regulatory compliance and the transition away from
fossil-based to bio-based materials."

The timing isn't incidental: the EU's Packaging and Packaging Waste
Regulation requires all packaging to be recyclable by 2030, and bioplastics
have sat awkwardly outside most existing recycling streams. Pilot scale
still means further scale-up and commercial validation ahead — this is a
proof, not yet a factory. But proving that a technology works in an
operating environment, sorting real waste rather than a curated sample, is
usually the harder half of the argument.
