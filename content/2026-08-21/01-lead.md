For twenty years, two ways of understanding diamond's most extreme behavior have
quietly disagreed with each other. Computer simulations built on quantum
mechanics predicted one thing; laboratory measurements of shocked diamond
kept returning something about 20 percent off. Neither side could explain
the gap. This week, a team at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory closed
it, and the fix turns out to matter for both fusion power and the physics of
the outer solar system
([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/08/260820002424.htm)).

The experiment itself is almost absurdly violent. Researchers fired intense
laser pulses at tiny diamond samples at the Omega Laser Facility, part of the
University of Rochester's Laboratory for Laser Energetics. The pulses
vaporize a diamond's outer layer, and the recoil sends a shockwave slamming
through the rest of the sample, compressing it to between 600 gigapascals
and 1.8 terapascals — roughly three times the pressure at Earth's core, and
higher than conditions deep inside Neptune or Uranus — while heating it
past the temperature of the sun's surface. Amid all that, the team used
X-ray diffraction to catch the diamond's atomic structure in the act of
rearranging itself, a measurement that had to be made in about a billionth
of a second.

What they found was that previous experiments had been misjudging the
temperature inside shocked diamond by more than 1,000 degrees Fahrenheit —
enough, on its own, to explain years of mismatch with the simulations. Once
corrected, the new measurements lined up almost perfectly with
quantum-mechanical models. The team also settled a related question about
how diamond melts under this kind of pressure: rather than passing through
some intermediate phase, it stays crystalline right up until it melts
completely, and the resulting liquid carbon is actually denser than the
solid diamond floating in it — the reverse of water and ice, but the same
basic idea of a solid phase riding atop its own melt. The results are
published in Nature Physics
([DOI: 10.1038/s41567-026-03413-1](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41567-026-03413-1)).

The practical stakes are real on two fronts. Inertial confinement fusion
experiments, of the kind pursued at facilities like the National Ignition
Facility, use diamond capsules to hold the fuel that gets imploded by lasers.
Simulations built on the newly corrected physics suggest that using a
slower initial shock — one that still fully melts the diamond shell rather
than leaving it partially solid — could triple the energy gain from a given
implosion, a meaningful lever in the long push toward fusion facilities that
put out more energy than they consume.

The other beneficiary is planetary science. Neptune and Uranus are thought
to harbor layers where immense pressure crushes methane into carbon, which
then crystallizes into diamond and rains slowly toward each planet's core —
a process that helps explain their lopsided, offset magnetic fields and
their internal heat budgets. Models of those interiors have had to guess at
exactly how diamond behaves under those conditions; now they have
experimental ground truth instead. It is a tidy example of how a stubborn,
narrow disagreement in a physics lab — a 20 percent gap nobody could
explain — can turn out to be worth resolving twice over, once for the
reactors we are trying to build and once for the planets we already have.
