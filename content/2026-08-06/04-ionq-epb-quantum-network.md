Quantum networking research has mostly happened in controlled lab
environments, disconnected from the fiber that actually carries the
world's data. IonQ and Chattanooga utility EPB are trying something
more exposed: a new Tennessee Quantum Communications Research Center
that installs what the partners describe as the world's first
commercial quantum memory unit embedded directly within an
operational communications network, rather than a sealed-off
demonstration rig
([IonQ](https://www.ionq.com/news/ionq-and-epb-partner-to-launch-the-tennessee-quantum-communications-research-center);
[The Quantum Insider](https://thequantuminsider.com/2026/08/04/ionq-epb-tennessee-quantum-communications-research-center/)).

IonQ is committing $15 million over five years, staffing the center
with its own scientists. EPB's role is arguably the more unusual
contribution: the utility, which already runs Chattanooga's
municipal fiber network, is opening up a live test bed rather than a
simulated one, and taking on the job of translating the lab's
research into real commercial and public-sector use cases. "A
Quantum Memory R&D capability that sits atop a working fiber optic
network will speed the development and deployment of innovative
quantum technologies," said IonQ CEO Niccolo de Masi.

The technical goal is quantum memory that can reliably hold and relay
quantum information across a real network, a missing piece for
linking distant quantum computers or securing communications with
quantum-based encryption — capabilities that have mostly existed as
short-range, lab-bench demonstrations until now. The partners
estimate the project will generate two to three times its own
investment in wider economic impact for the region, alongside roughly
two dozen research and support jobs.

It's a five-year commitment to a still-experimental technology, and
"the world's first" claims in quantum computing are common enough to
warrant some patience before judging the result. But testing quantum
networking against the mess of a live, operating network — rather
than a pristine lab setup — is exactly the kind of unglamorous step
the field needs before quantum communication becomes something more
than a research curiosity.
