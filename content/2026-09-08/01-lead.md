Lithium-manganese-rich batteries have been a tempting, frustrating idea
for a decade. Swap most of a battery's cobalt and nickel for manganese —
cheap, abundant, and not mined under the ethical shadow cobalt carries —
and you get a cathode that can, in theory, pack in more energy for less
money. In practice, LMR cells have a habit of producing gas as they age,
swelling the pouch and shortening the life of the cell. That single flaw
has kept the chemistry out of production EVs even as researchers have
chased it for years.

This week, a team led by Professor Jongwoo Lim at Seoul National
University's Department of Chemistry, working with LG Energy Solution,
reported in *Nature Communications* that the gas problem isn't only about
how high you charge the cell — it's also about how low you let it
discharge
([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/lg-energy-solution-and-seoul-national-university-open-the-door-to-commercializing-next-generation-lmr-batteries-302870957.html);
[Korea Herald](https://www.koreaherald.com/article/10865485)). Earlier
work had focused almost entirely on capping the charging voltage, since
LMR cathodes need a high voltage — historically around 4.6V — to unlock
the extra capacity manganese offers, and that high voltage is what
destabilizes oxygen in the material and releases gas. Lim's team found
that the bottom of the cycle mattered just as much: cells cycled with a
lower charge ceiling of 4.3V and discharged further down, to 2.0V instead
of the usual 3.0V, let the oxygen that had been oxidized during charging
properly return to its stable state afterward. That recovery rate rose
from 86% to 97%.

The difference showed up where it counts. Applied to real 40-amp-hour
large-format pouch cells — not lab coin cells, but the size actually used
in vehicle packs — the tuned protocol held 92.2% of the cell's original
capacity after 883 full charge-discharge cycles, a durability figure that
starts to look competitive with the nickel-heavy chemistries LMR is meant
to replace.

What makes the result notable is what it doesn't require. No new
electrolyte, no exotic coating, no change to how the cathode is
manufactured — just a different electrochemical protocol, the voltage
window a battery management system already controls. "Cell stability can
be improved through electrochemical protocol design alone," Lim said,
adding that long-term stability depends on considering charging and
discharging conditions together rather than tuning one in isolation. LG
Energy Solution, which is exploring commercial LMR production, called the
findings a way to address "one of the key challenges facing LMR
batteries" and said they show that stable battery life is achievable even
in large-format cells built at production scale.

It's a small dial to turn for a chemistry that could meaningfully lower
the cost of the next generation of EV and grid batteries — not because
someone invented a new material, but because someone finally looked
carefully at both ends of the cycle instead of just the top.
