---
title: A branched-nanowire LED could let light out instead of trapping it inside
kicker: Science
author: The Editors
summary: Lund University researchers built an LED thin enough that light can't get trapped by internal reflection, a fix for a decades-old efficiency problem that today requires expensive coatings.
order: 2
---

Most of the light a commercial LED generates never reaches your eyes. The
semiconductor material is denser than air, so light hitting its surface at
the wrong angle bounces back inside rather than escaping — total internal
reflection, the same effect that lets a swimming pool's surface act as a
mirror from underwater. Manufacturers fight it today with etched or
textured surface coatings, an extra manufacturing step that adds cost.
Researchers at Lund University, led by Professor Magnus Borgström, say
they've found a structural way around the problem instead: build the LED
out of nanowires too thin for the trap to work in the first place
([Lund University](https://www.lunduniversity.lu.se/article/new-type-led-light-could-bring-significant-efficiency-gains)).

The idea, described in the journal Nano Research with doctoral student Yue
Zhao as co-author, is that internal reflection depends on the material
being thick relative to the wavelength of the light passing through it.
Shrink the structure below that threshold — thinner than the wavelength
itself — and the usual rules of reflection stop applying. "If the
structures are made thin enough — thinner than the wavelength of light —
the light cannot be trapped inside the material in the same way,"
Borgström said. The team built their prototype from thin, branched
nanowires designed to exploit exactly that.

The scale of the potential gain is what makes this worth watching: the
team estimates ordinary commercial LEDs release only about 4 percent of
the light they generate without extra surface treatment. A structure that
lets nearly all of it out — without the added coating step — would be
both more efficient and, over time, cheaper to manufacture, since
nanowire structures use less raw material than conventional LED chips.

The caveats are the ordinary ones for early-stage materials science: this
is a lab demonstration, not a product, and the team's own next steps are
about closing the gap between theory and practice — refining the
nanowires further and reducing light loss from defects at their surfaces.
Still, an approach that removes a manufacturing step rather than adding
one is the kind of efficiency gain that tends to survive the trip from
lab bench to factory floor.
