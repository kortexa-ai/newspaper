---
title: An exoplanet-hunting trick just found a moon hiding next to an asteroid
kicker: Space
author: The Editors
summary: Astronomers borrowed techniques built for spotting distant exoplanets to pull a tiny, previously unseen moon out of the glare of the asteroid Nysa, and found signs the asteroid itself may be three bodies fused into one.
order: 4
---

Finding a one-kilometer moon next to an asteroid 150 million miles away
is mostly a problem of glare: the moon is faint, the asteroid is bright,
and the two sit close enough together that ordinary imaging just blurs
them into one blob of light. A team led by Kate Minker at Lowell
Observatory solved it by borrowing tools built for a different job
entirely — the adaptive-optics and image-processing techniques
exoplanet hunters use to tease a faint planet out from beside its
blazing host star.

Pointed at Nysa, one of the brightest and largest asteroids in the main
belt, the technique worked. Using the SHARK-VIS instrument on the Large
Binocular Telescope in Arizona and SPHERE/ZIMPOL on the Very Large
Telescope in Chile, the team spotted a roughly kilometer-wide moon,
designated S/2026 (44) 1, orbiting at least 170 kilometers out — and
caught it independently in two separate observing runs, on two different
telescopes, which is what turns a hint into a discovery
([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.25786)).

The moon itself is the more immediately useful find: tracking its orbit
will let astronomers weigh Nysa precisely, settling whether it's a solid
chunk of rock or a loosely bound rubble pile, a distinction that matters
for understanding how the early solar system's building blocks came
together. But the images also turned up something stranger — evidence
that Nysa itself isn't one body but three, welded together into what the
researchers describe as a contact trinary, with two valleys marking where
the lobes meet. If confirmed, it would be the first contact trinary
asteroid on record. The findings are posted as a preprint and have not
yet completed peer review, but the moon detection itself, confirmed
across two independent telescopes, stands on its own. It's a reminder
that the instruments built to hunt planets around other stars still have
plenty to find much closer to home.
