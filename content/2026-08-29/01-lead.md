NASA's next big space telescope has one more sunrise before launch. On
Friday, agency managers and their partners at SpaceX completed the Launch
Readiness Review for the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope — the final
sign-off before a mission moves into countdown — and concluded that "Falcon
Heavy, the Roman observatory, weather, and supporting teams are ready for
liftoff"
([NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/roman/2026/08/28/nasas-roman-space-telescope-go-for-launch/)).
Liftoff is set for Sunday, August 30, at 7:26 a.m. EDT from Launch Complex
39A at Kennedy Space Center, atop a SpaceX Falcon Heavy, with forecasters
putting the odds of acceptable weather at 60 percent
([NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/roman/2026/08/28/nasas-roman-space-telescope-go-for-launch/)).

It's a mission with an unusually broad job description. Roman carries a
mirror the same size as Hubble's, but paired with a camera built to see a
field of view roughly a hundred times wider, letting it survey huge swaths
of sky in a single pointing rather than the narrow, deep stares Hubble and
Webb specialize in. NASA describes the telescope's task list as mapping
billions of galaxies, hunting and characterizing exoplanets, tracing the
gravitational fingerprints of dark matter, and probing dark energy — the
mysterious force accelerating the universe's expansion — all from data it
will make freely available to scientists and the public
([NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/roman/2026/08/28/nasas-roman-space-telescope-go-for-launch/)).
The telescope is named for Nancy Grace Roman, NASA's first chief
astronomer, who helped shepherd Hubble itself from proposal to launch
decades earlier.

Sunday's flight will be the sixth NASA science mission to fly on a Falcon
rocket out of Kennedy Space Center, a track record that has made SpaceX's
heaviest operational launcher a routine ride for the agency's most
ambitious payloads
([NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/roman/2026/08/28/nasas-roman-space-telescope-go-for-launch/)).
Getting to this point took the review process NASA runs before every major
launch — a formal accounting of the hardware, the software, the weather
models and the teams on console, meant to catch any reason to wait before,
not after, the countdown clock starts.

None of that guarantees a clean Sunday morning; weather and rockets both
reserve the right to change plans. But with the review board's sign-off in
hand, Roman is as close as a space telescope gets to ready: fueled,
tested, reviewed, and one good launch window away from a mission designed
to spend the next several years quietly rewriting how much of the
universe astronomers can see at once.
