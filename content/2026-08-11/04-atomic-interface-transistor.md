title: Engineers find the missing layer that could keep chips shrinking
kicker: Infrastructure
author: The Editors
summary: A joint TSMC and university team solved a stubborn trade-off in atom-thin transistors by inserting a deliberately engineered interface just 0.42 nanometers thick — a step toward chips built from materials thinner than silicon allows.
order: 4
---

As transistors shrink toward the width of a few atoms, silicon starts
running out of room — its own physics limits how thin its insulating
layers can get before current leaks through uncontrollably. Molybdenum
disulfide, a semiconductor just one atomic layer thick, has long been a
leading candidate to take over, but pairing it with a thin, reliable
insulating layer without wrecking its electrical performance has been a
persistent obstacle.

Researchers at National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University and TSMC
Corporate Research, led by professors Wen-Hao Chang and Tsung-En Lee,
found a way through: depositing an ultrathin layer of aluminum directly
onto monolayer molybdenum disulfide and carefully oxidizing it into just
0.42 nanometers of aluminum oxide, before adding a hafnium oxide gate
dielectric on top. That thin buffer layer turned out to solve two problems
at once — it gives the hafnium oxide a smooth, even surface to grow on, and
it shields the semiconductor from electrical interference that would
otherwise scatter its charge carriers
([EurekAlert!](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1138925)).

The resulting transistors, built with commercially relevant CVD-grown
material rather than hand-flaked lab samples, showed low leakage current,
minimal hysteresis, and a maximum transconductance of 0.45 milliseconds
per micrometer on 100-nanometer channels — a combination the team says is
rarely achieved together in atom-thin devices. The work was published July
31 in Nature Electronics. "When transistor components become only a few
atomic layers thick, the interface is no longer simply the boundary
between materials, it becomes an active part of the device," said Lee.
Because the method works with wafer-scale-compatible material growth
rather than exfoliated flakes, it's a real step toward manufacturing —
not just a lab curiosity — for chips built from materials thinner than
silicon allows.
