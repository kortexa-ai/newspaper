title: A future telescope might spot alien oceans by catching their sunset shimmer
kicker: Space
author: The Editors
summary: University of Arizona researchers worked out the viewing angle a future observatory would need to catch "ocean glint" — the same shimmer sunlight makes on Earth's seas — reflecting off a distant Earth-like planet.
order: 3
---

Anyone who has watched a sunset over open water knows the effect: a bright, mirror-like glare where sunlight bounces directly off the surface. It turns out that same "glint" could be one of the more practical ways to confirm liquid water on a planet light-years away, according to a paper posted September 4 by Eleanor Cornish and Tyler Robinson at the University of Arizona ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04597); [Universe Today](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/nasas-next-great-observatory-could-spot-oceans-on-distant-worlds)).

Detecting an ocean on a planet too distant to see anything but a single pixel of light is a genuinely hard problem — you can't watch waves, only measure how brightness changes with the geometry of star, planet, and telescope. The researchers modeled how NASA's planned Habitable Worlds Observatory would see reflected starlight from an Earth-like exoplanet at different points in its orbit, adding realistic wind and wave behavior to the simulation. They found that at a "crescent phase" — a viewing angle of at least 120 degrees between star and telescope — the glint signature becomes distinct enough to reliably separate ocean worlds from dry ones, with an added reddening effect from atmospheric scattering serving as a confirming clue.

It's a modeling paper, not a discovery — there's no ocean-bearing planet found here, only a clearer map of when one could be. But that's exactly the kind of unglamorous groundwork a mission still on the drawing board needs: a concrete viewing-angle requirement that telescope designers can build toward now, years before Habitable Worlds Observatory ever gets built and pointed at the sky. Clouds could still complicate things, either hiding an ocean or faking its glint — a caveat the authors flag as their main limitation. But knowing what to look for, and from where, is how you eventually go looking.
