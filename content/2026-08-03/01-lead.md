Plastic recycling has a sorting problem. Bottles, bags, and food
containers are made of different polymers that melt and burn
differently, so most "recycling" plants either can't process a mixed
bale or reduce it to lower-grade material through slow, wasteful
sorting. A team led by Ah-Hyung "Alissa" Park, dean of the UCLA
Samueli School of Engineering, and Woo-Jae Kim of Ewha Womans
University in Seoul, has now shown a way around that entirely: feed
the mixed plastic in as-is, and get clean hydrogen fuel out, with most
of the carbon staying put as solid rock rather than escaping into the
air.

The process, described July 31 in the *Proceedings of the National
Academy of Sciences*
([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/07/260729051521.htm);
[phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-07-plastic-hydrogen-fuel.html)),
is called alkaline thermal treatment. It works by heating a mix of the
three most common consumer plastics — PET, polyethylene, and
polypropylene — together with sodium hydroxide, the same alkaline
compound used in drain cleaner. Instead of burning or gasifying the
plastic in the usual way, the alkali reacts with the material as it
breaks down, pulling carbon out of the vapor stream and locking it
into solid sodium carbonate before it ever has a chance to become
carbon dioxide. What comes off is hydrogen gas at better than 90
percent purity.

The chemistry solves two problems that are usually treated as separate
projects. One is that mixed, contaminated plastic waste is exactly the
kind of material that conventional recycling struggles with — no
sorting line required here, which matters because sorting is where a
lot of real-world plastic recycling programs quietly fail. The other
is that most industrial hydrogen production today still runs on
natural gas and releases carbon dioxide as a byproduct, undercutting
hydrogen's promise as a clean fuel before it's even burned. This
process runs 300 to 400 degrees Celsius cooler than traditional steam
gasification of plastic, and more than 75 percent of the plastic's
original carbon content ends up captured, either as stable solid
carbonate or as a liquid organic residue that doesn't reach the
atmosphere as gas.

"We are solving two urgent global problems at the same time," Park
said of the work. "Plastic waste that pollutes our land and oceans,
and the need for clean hydrogen fuel that doesn't add to climate
change... This technology tackles both challenges together in a
creative and scalable way."

The team, which also includes researchers from Korea Aerospace
University, Kangwon National University, and Sogang University, is
upfront that this is a laboratory result, not a factory. Scaling the
alkaline thermal treatment to handle real-world tonnage, recovering
and reusing the sodium hydroxide economically, and running the safety
and cost studies that any new industrial chemical process requires
are all still ahead. But the core result — plastic in, hydrogen and
rock out, without a sorting line and without most of the carbon
reaching the sky — is the kind of unglamorous chemistry that, if it
scales, could make two hard problems a little easier at once.
