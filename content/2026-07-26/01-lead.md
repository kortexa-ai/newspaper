Soyuz MS-28 touched down on the Kazakh steppe southeast of Dzhezkazgan at
5:27 a.m. CDT on Sunday, closing out a 241-day mission with the kind of
ending every spaceflight hopes for: on schedule, on target, parachutes
open, everyone in good health
([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-astronaut-chris-williams-crewmates-return-from-space-station/);
corroborated by [Space.com](https://www.space.com/space-exploration/human-spaceflight/nasa-astronaut-two-cosmonauts-return-to-earth-soyuz-ms-28)).
Aboard were NASA astronaut Chris Williams and Roscosmos cosmonauts Sergey
Kud-Sverchkov and Sergei Mikaev — a crew that had launched together on
November 27, 2025, orbited the Earth 3,856 times, and covered more than
102 million miles before Sunday's descent.

For Williams and Mikaev, it was a first flight; for Kud-Sverchkov, a
second. What made the mission more than a long stay in orbit was the
work Williams did outside the station. He conducted two spacewalks
during Expedition 74: one to support upgrades to the station's power
system, and a second, trickier one to replace a faulty joint on
Canadarm2, the station's robotic arm
([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-astronaut-chris-williams-crewmates-return-from-space-station/)).
Canadarm2 is not a convenience — it's the arm that captures visiting
cargo ships, repositions station modules, and gives astronauts a mobile
work platform for exterior repairs. An arm with a failing joint is a
maintenance problem that eventually becomes an operational one, and
fixing it from outside the station, in a suit, is the kind of task that
looks routine only after it has gone right.

None of this made headlines the way a rocket launch does. That's
somewhat the point of a space station eleven years into continuous
operation: its biggest wins are increasingly about keeping aging,
essential hardware working, not about spectacle. A robotic arm repaired
by hand in vacuum is unglamorous in exactly the way that a reliable,
25-year-old orbital laboratory has to be.

Back on Earth, the choreography was its own small marvel of routine
excellence. After medical checks on the steppe, recovery teams flew the
crew by helicopter to Karaganda, Kazakhstan, where Williams then boarded
a NASA aircraft bound for Johnson Space Center in Houston — a handoff
between two space agencies and two countries that, however geopolitically
complicated, has worked smoothly for a quarter century of Soyuz landings.

Expedition 74's departure leaves a new crew aboard the station to carry
its work forward, part of NASA's broader effort to keep the outpost
running while the agency's attention increasingly turns toward the Moon
and, eventually, Mars. But for one Sunday morning, the news was simpler
than any of that: three people who left Earth in November came back
safely in July, and the station's arm works again because one of them
went outside and fixed it.
