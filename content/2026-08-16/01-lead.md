Brazil's National Institute for Space Research reported this month that its
DETER alert system detected 2,874 square kilometers of Amazon deforestation
between August 2025 and July 2026 — a 36 percent drop from the previous
12-month period and 55.6 percent below the average of the preceding decade.
It is the lowest total in the alert system's record, a signal, cautiously
read, that one of the planet's most consequential environmental fights is
still winnable
([Mongabay](https://news.mongabay.com/2026/08/amazon-deforestation-alerts-fall-to-lowest-level-since-2013-brazilian-data-show/);
[IPAM Amazônia](https://ipam.org.br/decline-in-alerts-paves-the-way-for-zero-deforestation-in-the-amazon/)).

The state-by-state numbers tell a more textured story than the headline
figure. Mato Grosso, long one of the hardest-hit agricultural frontiers, saw
alerts fall 49 percent; Amazonas dropped 46.7 percent; Rondônia fell 41.2
percent; Acre declined 35.3 percent. Pará, the state that has recorded the
most forest loss in absolute terms for years, improved by a more modest 25.5
percent. And Roraima went the other way entirely, with alerts up 32.5
percent — a reminder that national trends can mask real regional setbacks,
and that this is a fight being won unevenly, not a switch that got flipped.

"The Deter data indicate a sustained reduction in deforestation in the
Amazon, which is very positive," said Ane Alencar, director of science at
IPAM, the Amazon Environmental Research Institute, adding that the trend
keeps Brazil on a plausible path toward its stated goal of zero
deforestation by 2030.

Credit, by most independent accounts, goes to enforcement rather than
market forces or weather. Since President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva
returned to office in 2023, Brazil's environmental agencies have ramped up
field operations against illegal clearing, resumed demarcation of
Indigenous territories — which tend to be far better protected than
surrounding land — and restored funding and political backing that had
been stripped away under the previous administration.

Two caveats are worth stating plainly, in keeping with how cautiously this
kind of data should be read. DETER is a near-real-time alert system built
to guide enforcement, not Brazil's official annual deforestation count;
that figure comes from a separate, higher-resolution system called PRODES,
whose results for this period are expected in October or November. If
PRODES confirms the DETER trend, Brazil could report its lowest official
Amazon deforestation rate since satellite monitoring began in 1988, not
merely since 2013. And clear-cutting is only one way a forest gets
damaged — selective logging, drought, and fire can degrade the Amazon's
ecology substantially without ever showing up as deforestation in these
statistics.

None of that diminishes what the numbers show. A forest the size of a small
country was left standing this year that recent history says would
otherwise have come down, and it happened because a government decided,
concretely and repeatedly, to enforce the law.
