The Navier-Stokes equations describe how fluids move — water in a pipe, air over a wing — and for nearly a century mathematicians have been unable to answer a basic question about them: does a perfectly smooth three-dimensional flow always stay smooth, or can it break down into a singularity, a point where the equations stop making sense? The Clay Mathematics Institute made it one of seven Millennium Prize Problems in 2000, each worth $1 million, precisely because nobody could prove it either way.

On September 8, researchers at OpenAI announced they had found such a singularity — a configuration where a vortex tightens and spins faster without bound while its energy stays finite — resolving the question. Unusually for a mathematical claim announced this fast, the result was formalized and checked in the Lean proof assistant, giving it machine-verified certainty rather than resting on human review alone
([Quanta Magazine](https://www.quantamagazine.org/ai-has-solved-one-of-maths-1-million-millennium-prize-problems-20260908/);
[Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-02842-5)).

The genuine breakthrough, though, predates the announcement by years and belongs to two mathematicians: Diego Córdoba and Luis Martínez-Zoroa, whose technique for building an "infinite cascade" of smooth, non-singular layers that combine into a singularity gave both this result and a closely related one their opening. Princeton's Charles Fefferman, one of the field's senior figures, was unambiguous about credit: "The heroes of the story... are Córdoba and Martínez-Zoroa." Tristan Buckmaster, a mathematician at NYU who had been working with Levent Alpöge on a related singularity in the Euler equations — a sibling problem without its own Millennium Prize — went further, saying Martínez-Zoroa "deserves a Fields Medal."

That two teams arrived at closely related results within about half a day of each other, one built by an AI lab racing to demonstrate its own model and one by mathematicians who had been quietly building toward theirs, has stirred real debate in the field about credit, timing, and how mathematics gets done when AI systems can search a proof space at a scale no human team can match. It's a conversation worth having honestly. It doesn't change the underlying fact: a question that outlasted generations of mathematicians now has a checked answer, and the foundational insight that cracked it came from two people who worked the problem the old way, one idea at a time.
