title: A dead star's magnetic field may have caught empty space bending light
kicker: Space
author: The Editors
summary: Coordinated X-ray and radio observations of a rare magnetar have turned up the strongest evidence yet for vacuum birefringence, a quantum effect Werner Heisenberg predicted nearly 90 years ago.
order: 2
---

Werner Heisenberg predicted, almost 90 years ago, that a strong enough
magnetic field should be able to bend light passing through what physicists
call a vacuum — otherwise, truly empty space. An international team,
publishing in *Nature* on August 5, has now reported the clearest sign yet
that this effect, called vacuum birefringence, is real
([Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-08-rare-ultra-magnetic-star-key.html);
[CSIRO](https://www.csiro.au/en/news/all/news/2026/august/rare-ultra-magnetic-star-the-key-to-solving-a-quantum-cold-case);
[Swinburne University](https://www.swinburne.edu.au/news/2026/08/rare-ultra-magnetic-star-the-key-to-solving-a-quantum-cold-case/)).

The idea rests on quantum electrodynamics: even a vacuum isn't truly
empty, but a shifting sea of virtual particles that flicker in and out of
existence. In a magnetic field powerful enough, those virtual particles
should polarize the vacuum itself, bending the light that passes through it
in a detectable way. Proving it requires a magnetic field far beyond
anything achievable on Earth, so the team turned to magnetar
1E 1547.0-5408, a neutron star with a magnetic field roughly a trillion
times stronger than Earth's, spinning once every 2.1 seconds. By combining,
for the first time, coordinated X-ray and radio polarization measurements of
the same magnetar, the researchers found polarization far higher than
expected — 40% in the star's upper radiation beam and 80% in the lower one,
nearly three times the level seen in comparable objects.

The result doesn't close the case; the team describes it as the strongest
evidence so far rather than definitive proof, and further observations will
be needed to rule out other explanations. But it's a reminder that some of
the most direct tests of fundamental quantum theory now come not from
particle accelerators but from turning telescopes toward the most extreme
objects the universe has to offer.
