---
title: Antarctica just got a lot easier to forecast
kicker: Science
author: The Editors
summary: A new modeling study finds Antarctic ice loss is reliably predictable out to mid-century, handing planners a rare, defined window to prepare for sea level rise.
order: 4
---

Planning for sea level rise has always run into the same problem:
Antarctica's ice sheet is the biggest wild card in the forecast, and wild
cards are hard to plan around. A study published July 23 in *Nature*
offers planners something they haven't had before — a defined stretch of
time in which the ice sheet's behavior is actually predictable
([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/07/260722032123.htm),
corroborated by [SciTechDaily](https://scitechdaily.com/antarcticas-future-may-be-more-predictable-than-scientists-thought/)
and [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-06-antarctica-years-worth-sea.html)).

Led by Dr. Felicity McCormack of Monash University and Securing
Antarctica's Environmental Future, the team tested how well ice sheet
models could project decades forward once anchored to observed,
present-day conditions. The result: when a model accurately reproduces
the rate of ice loss Antarctica is showing right now, that same model
reliably predicts its contribution to sea level rise for roughly the next
30 to 50 years — regardless of which emissions pathway the world ends up
on. "If ice sheet models accurately reproduce the rates of ice loss we
observe today, we can have confidence in using those same models to
reliably predict Antarctica's contribution to sea level rise over the
next 30 to 50 years," McCormack said.

That predictability doesn't last forever — past roughly mid-century, the
risk of unstable, harder-to-model retreat processes taking over grows,
and the forecast window closes. But a 30-to-50-year runway is exactly the
kind of timeframe coastal cities, insurers, and infrastructure planners
need and rarely get from climate science, which more often deals in
ranges and caveats than usable dates. The finding doesn't make the
underlying risk smaller. It makes the risk something governments can
actually build a plan around, starting now, while the window is open.
