---
title: Starship's upper stage survived splashdown intact for the first time
kicker: Space
author: The Editors
summary: Flight 13's ship nailed a soft Indian Ocean landing, deployed satellites, and relit an engine in space — even as its booster had a rougher day.
order: 2
---

SpaceX's thirteenth Starship test flight, launched July 25 from
Starbase, Texas, delivered the kind of mixed, honest result that
actually tells you something: real progress on the vehicle that matters
most for the program's future, alongside a reminder that the booster
still has work to do
([Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/2026/07/25/super-heavy-starship-rocket-chalks-up-mostly-successful-test-flight/)).

All 33 Raptor engines on the Super Heavy booster fired cleanly at
liftoff, but the booster ran into trouble on its way back down — only
10 of 13 engines meant to relight for landing did, and just five were
still running at splashdown, resulting in a hard impact in the Gulf of
Mexico rather than a controlled touchdown. The upper stage told a
different story. All six of its engines ran smoothly through ascent, it
deployed 20 third-generation Starlink satellites, briefly relit an
engine in space to prove out the restart capability future lunar
missions will depend on, and — for the first time in the program's
history — splashed down in the Indian Ocean intact rather than breaking
apart or exploding on impact. Six of the deployed satellites carried
cameras specifically to inspect the ship's heat shield during re-entry,
data SpaceX has been chasing for years
([Space.com](https://www.space.com/space-exploration/launches-spacecraft/spacexs-starship-megarocket-makes-the-softest-splashdown-ever-after-launching-next-gen-starlink-satellites-in-flight-13-test-video)).

The landing was gentle enough that the ship was still floating off the
coast of Western Australia five days later, transmitting telemetry the
whole time — an unplanned bonus of engineering data that a violent
breakup would never have provided
([Space.com photo of the day, July 29](https://www.space.com/space-exploration/launches-spacecraft/spacex-starship-still-floating-5-days-after-flight-space-photo-of-the-day-for-july-29-2026)).
NASA is watching closely: a Starship variant is slated to be the lunar
lander for Artemis, targeting a 2028 landing, and the Government
Accountability Office has flagged propellant-management and schedule
risk on that timeline. A test flight that gets the booster wrong and the
ship mostly right isn't a clean win. But the ship is the harder, more
important half of the vehicle to get flying reliably, and this is the
first time it has come home in one piece.
