---
title: A swap to krypton gas could make quantum chips easier to manufacture
kicker: Science
author: The Editors
summary: Cornell researchers found that sputtering tantalum with krypton instead of argon cuts the temperature needed to make high-quality qubit films in half, clearing a path toward building them on ordinary chip-fab equipment.
order: 2
---

Superconducting quantum computers need thin films of tantalum that are
close to flawless, and until now growing them required heating silicon
wafers past 400°C — hot enough to damage the control circuitry that
commercial chip factories build everything else around. Researchers at
Cornell, led by assistant professor Valla Fatemi with postdoctoral
researcher Maciej Olszewski as lead author, found a fix hiding in plain
sight: swap the argon gas normally used in the deposition process for
krypton, and the same high-quality tantalum forms at just 200°C
([Cornell Chronicle](https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2026/08/new-ingredient-quantum-computing-krypton-gas);
[Nature Materials](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41563-026-02718-z)).

The technique, published in *Nature Materials*, is a small tweak to a
process called magnetron sputtering, in which a gas plasma knocks tantalum
atoms loose from a target so they can settle onto a silicon wafer in an
ordered crystal structure. Krypton atoms are heavier than argon's, which
turns out to matter: they carry more momentum, and momentum is what argon
had been supplying with heat. Films grown this way had substantially higher
electronic conductivity than earlier low-temperature attempts, and transmon
qubits fabricated from them — with capacitor gaps as small as 20
micrometers — reached internal quality factors up to 16.9 million, on par
with the best qubits made the old, hotter way.

That 200°C threshold is the practical breakthrough. It falls within the
temperature budget that commercial semiconductor foundries already work
within to avoid frying the CMOS control electronics and interconnect layers
that sit beneath the qubits, meaning fabs built for conventional chips could
plausibly run this process without new tooling. "The process is now
translatable to nanofabrication systems in industry," Fatemi said, while
still landing qubits at the "world-leading edge" of performance. Every
manufacturing constraint removed between a quantum processor and an
existing chip fab is one less reason building larger ones stays confined to
a handful of specialized academic and government labs — and one more reason
it might not.
