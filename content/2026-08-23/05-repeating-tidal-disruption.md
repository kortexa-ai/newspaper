---
title: Some stars keep surviving the black hole that keeps eating them
kicker: Science
author: The Editors
summary: Astronomers have identified about ten stars caught in repeat close encounters with supermassive black holes, flaring again and again instead of being destroyed in one pass.
order: 5
---

When a star strays too close to a supermassive black hole, the standard
story ends with the star torn apart in a single violent flare. A growing
body of evidence says that is not always how it ends. Researchers led by
Syracuse University doctoral student Ananya Bandopadhyay, postdoctoral
researcher Benjamin Amend, and associate professor Eric Coughlin have
catalogued roughly ten "repeating partial tidal disruption events," in
which a star grazes a black hole, loses only part of itself, survives, and
returns months or years later to be stripped again — sometimes for multiple
recorded passes ([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/08/260822015119.htm)).

The study, published in The Astrophysical Journal
([DOI: 10.3847/1538-4357/ae8f31](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/ae8f31)),
tackles a puzzle that had stumped the team for two years: in four of the
repeating systems, each successive flare from the same star is dimmer than
the last, rather than roughly the same brightness. "We were puzzled by this
for two years," Bandopadhyay said. Using hydrodynamical simulations, the
team now thinks the answer lies in how fast the star was spinning before it
was ever captured — stars that were already rotating rapidly lose mass more
gradually with each pass, which would explain both the fading flares and
how such stars ended up locked into tight, repeating orbits around a black
hole in the first place.

"From a theoretical standpoint, this is a major step forward in our
understanding of the physics at play in these systems," said Coughlin. The
finding reframes what a tidal disruption flare actually represents: not
necessarily a star's death notice, but potentially the opening chapter of a
much longer relationship between a star and the black hole slowly wearing
it down, one close pass at a time.
