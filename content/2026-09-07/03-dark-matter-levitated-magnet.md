---
title: A floating grain-sized magnet just widened the search for dark matter
kicker: Science
author: The Editors
summary: Rice University physicists used a magnetically levitated particle, sensitive to motion a hundredth the width of an atom, to hunt for dark matter particles far heavier than any prior levitation experiment could catch.
order: 3
---

Most dark matter searches are built to catch light, fast-moving particles.
A team led by Christopher Tunnell at Rice University took the opposite
bet: build a detector sensitive enough to notice something enormous and
slow instead, a class of hypothetical "ultraheavy" dark matter particles —
potentially as massive as a living cell — that conventional experiments
were never designed to reach
([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-09-levitated-magnet-frontier-ultraheavy-dark.html)).

Their detector, run in a lab in Leiden, the Netherlands, is disarmingly
simple in concept: a tiny magnet, about the size of a grain of sand,
levitated above a superconductor cooled to near absolute zero. With
nothing touching it, the magnet is free to register the faintest push —
the kind a passing ultraheavy particle might deliver as it collided with
ordinary matter. The setup is sensitive to movement roughly a hundredth
the width of an atom, precise enough that after about a month of data
collection, the team could rule out particle interactions across nine
orders of magnitude of mass, extending the search to particles some 10
million times heavier than previous levitated-particle experiments could
probe.

"Dark matter could be hiding at masses that our traditional experiments
were never built to reach," said Tunnell, an associate professor of
physics and astronomy. Postdoctoral researcher Juehang Qin, describing the
detection strategy, put it more simply: "Instead of looking for a steady
signal, we are waiting for very small knocks."

No knocks arrived. The month-long run found no evidence of ultraheavy dark
matter, and the team is careful about what that does and doesn't mean —
the result sets new upper limits on how strongly such particles could
interact with normal matter, not proof that they don't exist. That's the
ordinary shape of progress in a field where the target has never been
directly observed: each experiment narrows the map of where it could be
hiding, even when it comes home empty-handed. A floating magnet the size
of a grain of sand has just quietly redrawn a piece of that map.
