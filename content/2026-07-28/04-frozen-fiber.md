---
title: Physicists froze a fiber and got 1,000 times more signal
kicker: Science
author: The Editors
summary: German researchers found that freezing a liquid-core optical fiber to -196°C makes light and sound interact far more strongly — a boost that could cut the energy cost of photonic computing.
order: 4
---

Researchers at the Max Planck Institute for the Science of Light, Leibniz
University Hannover, and the Leibniz Institute for Photonic Technologies
built a fiber that gets more useful the colder it gets. By chilling a
liquid-core optical fiber to -196°C in liquid nitrogen, the team — led by
Simon Seiderer in Birgit Stiller's Quantum Optoacoustics group — froze the
liquid core solid, and found that the frozen section coupled light and
sound waves more than 1,000 times more strongly than a standard glass
fiber ([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-07-frozen-fiber-couples-strongly-standard.html)).

The effect at work, Brillouin-Mandelstam scattering, is normally weak and
hard to exploit. The frozen, densely confined fiber core changes that,
and the team used the stronger coupling to demonstrate "optoacoustic
memory" — a way of storing a light signal as a sound wave inside the
fiber itself, a building block for photonic neuromorphic computing. The
work was published July 19 in *Optica*
(Seiderer et al., "Giant Brillouin gain in frozen CS₂ capillaries,"
DOI: 10.1364/optica.600056).

The near-term payoff isn't a product — it's efficiency. Photonic
computing and quantum signal processing both lean on light-sound
interactions that are normally so weak they burn through power just to
be usable. A fiber that does the same job with a thousand-fold stronger
signal means the same computation for a fraction of the energy, which is
exactly the kind of unglamorous physics that eventually shows up in
faster, cooler-running chips.
