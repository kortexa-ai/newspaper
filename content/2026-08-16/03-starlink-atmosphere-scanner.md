---
title: Researchers turned 1,200 Starlink satellites into a giant atmospheric scanner
kicker: Space
author: The Editors
summary: A Kyoto University team borrowed a medical-imaging technique to map Earth's thermosphere from nothing but publicly available Starlink orbital data, then checked their results against a dedicated ESA science satellite — and they matched.
order: 3
---

Nobody built these satellites to do atmospheric science. But a team led by
Mamoru Yamamoto at Kyoto University realized that the way Starlink
satellites' orbits quietly decay — dragged down by faint resistance from
the thermosphere, roughly 300 miles up — was itself a measurement, if you
knew how to read it. Using publicly available orbital data from about 1,200
satellites, they applied tomography, the technique behind CT scans, to
reconstruct the first two-dimensional, latitude-by-longitude map of
thermospheric density at that altitude
([Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-08-upper-atmosphere-starlink-satellite.html);
[ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/08/260812015212.htm)).
The study appeared August 4 in Earth, Planets and Space.

The thermosphere is more than 99 percent electrically neutral gas and
notoriously hard to observe directly — unlike the ionosphere just below it,
it doesn't carry a charge that instruments can easily pick up from the
ground. Dedicated science satellites like the European Space Agency's SWARM
mission measure it by flying through it, one narrow path at a time. Turning
a commercial mega-constellation into thousands of simultaneous drag sensors
gives researchers something SWARM alone can't: broad, near-continuous
coverage. When Yamamoto's team checked their tomographic map against
SWARM's direct measurements, the two agreed closely.

"This is a multidisciplinary study between space science and space
engineering," Yamamoto said of the approach. The practical case for caring
is growing by the week: as thousands more satellites crowd low Earth orbit,
better real-time maps of atmospheric density mean better collision
predictions and faster warnings when a solar storm swells the thermosphere
and drags satellites' orbits down unexpectedly. It's a tidy bit of
scientific frugality — squeezing a genuinely new instrument out of data
that was just sitting there, publicly, the whole time.
