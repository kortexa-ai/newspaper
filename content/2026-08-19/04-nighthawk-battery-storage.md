In Poway, California, a 1,200-megawatt-hour battery installation called
Nighthawk went into commercial operation this month, giving the San Diego
region's grid a substantial new buffer against peak demand. Developed,
built, and operated by Arevon Energy under a long-term agreement with
Pacific Gas and Electric, the 300-megawatt project is the largest standalone
battery in Arevon's portfolio and among the biggest storage facilities in
the region
([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/arevon-celebrates-construction-proceedings-at-its-300-megawatt1-200-megawatt-hour-nighthawk-energy-storage-project-in-california-302518268.html);
[Energy-Storage.News](https://www.energy-storage.news/arevon-energys-1200mwh-california-bess-begins-operations/);
[Morningstar](https://www.morningstar.com/news/pr-newswire/20260812ny24361/arevons-300-megawatt-nighthawk-energy-storage-project-is-operational-in-poway-california)).

Nighthawk works the way grid batteries increasingly do: it charges up when
electricity is cheap and plentiful, then discharges when demand spikes,
smoothing out the peaks that would otherwise strain the grid or require
firing up costlier backup generation. Built with lithium iron phosphate
cells, prized for their safety record and long service life, the facility
can supply enough power for up to 385,000 homes for as long as four hours
at a stretch. At peak construction it employed more than 130 workers, and
Arevon expects the project to generate over $30 million in property tax
revenue over its lifetime, money earmarked for local schools, public
safety, and infrastructure in the surrounding community.

There's nothing flashy about a battery in a field. But projects like
Nighthawk are the quiet infrastructure that makes a grid leaning more
heavily on solar and wind actually reliable around the clock — storing
midday abundance for the evening hours when it's needed most.
