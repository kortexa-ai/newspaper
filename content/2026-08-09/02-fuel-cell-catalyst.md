---
title: A better carbon scaffold lets fuel cells use a lot less platinum
kicker: Climate
author: The Editors
summary: Engineers at Washington University in St. Louis built a porous carbon support that keeps platinum catalyst particles small, ordered, and durable — even after the equivalent of 25,000 hours of operation.
order: 2
---

Platinum is the reason hydrogen fuel cells are still expensive. It's the
best catalyst known for the reaction that turns hydrogen and oxygen into
electricity and water, and it's also one of the rarest metals on Earth.
Every gram a fuel cell can shed, without losing performance, moves the
technology closer to competing with batteries and combustion on cost
alone.

A team led by Gang Wu at Washington University in St. Louis, working with
researchers at Brookhaven National Laboratory, Lawrence Berkeley National
Laboratory, Northeastern University, and the University of Pittsburgh,
built a new kind of support structure for the catalyst: hollow carbon
spheres threaded with ordered, radial nanochannels. That porous
architecture lets the platinum-cobalt nanoparticles inside be synthesized
at a scorching 1,000°C — hot enough to form a highly ordered atomic
structure, which is what makes a catalyst efficient — without the
particles fusing together into useless clumps, which is what usually
happens at that temperature ([WashU](https://source.washu.edu/2026/08/platinum-powers-the-future/)).

The payoff shows up in durability testing. The catalyst kept 85% of its
performance after 150,000 voltage cycles, equivalent to roughly 25,000
hours of real-world operation, while holding its platinum-cobalt
particles below 5 nanometers and evenly spread through the carbon
scaffold. That combination — high loading, small particles, long life —
has been difficult to get in one material; ordering usually costs you
durability. The research was published in *Nature Nanotechnology* on
August 6 ([DOI: 10.1038/s41565-026-02244-8](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41565-026-02244-8)).

The near-term target isn't cars. It's the power-hungry data centers now
straining electrical grids across the country, where a fuel cell that
runs longer between replacements starts to look like a genuine
alternative source of steady, on-site power. Wu described the process
plainly: the carbon support "could heat the platinum-cobalt catalyst to
1000°C, which is high enough to form a very ordered structure while still
keeping the nanoparticles smaller than 5 nanometers." It's the kind of
unglamorous materials engineering that doesn't reinvent a technology so
much as make the existing one considerably harder to argue against.
