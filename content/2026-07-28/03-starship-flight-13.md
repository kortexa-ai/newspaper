---
title: Starship's thirteenth flight nails its hardest job yet
kicker: Space
author: The Editors
summary: SpaceX's Starship deployed its first next-generation Starlink satellites and survived reentry more intact than ever before — even though its booster didn't stick the landing.
order: 3
---

SpaceX launched Starship on its thirteenth test flight from Starbase,
Texas, on July 24, and for the first time proved the vehicle can do the
job it's actually meant for: deploying working satellites in orbit
([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-07-spacex-starship-flight-advanced-starlinks.html);
[CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/07/24/science/live-news/spacex-starship-flight-13-launch)).

All 33 Raptor engines on the Super Heavy booster lit cleanly at liftoff,
and Starship reached its planned suborbital trajectory before releasing
20 next-generation Starlink V3 satellites at roughly 124 miles up. Each
satellite had only about 20 minutes to check in over laser and radio
links before Starship itself reentered the atmosphere — SpaceX confirmed
contact with, and downloaded data from, every one of them. Six of the
satellites carried cameras that caught the ship's heat shield during
reentry, described by engineers as "amazingly crisp shots." The upper
stage then splashed down upright in the Indian Ocean with minimal
flames, the most intact reentry and landing the vehicle has managed
across thirteen flights. "We got all the heat shield data we needed and
then some!" Elon Musk said afterward; SpaceX engineer Kate Tice called it
simply "lucky flight 13."

The booster's half of the flight went less smoothly: not enough engines
relit for the controlled return SpaceX was hoping for, and it came down
in the Gulf of Mexico faster than planned. That's a real gap to close
before Starship can fly as a routine, reusable Starlink launcher. But the
part that matters most for the program's future — proving the ship can
release working satellites and come home in one piece — just happened
for the first time.
