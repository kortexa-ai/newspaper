For twenty years, astronomers estimating how much a distant galaxy weighs
have relied on its brightest stars as a proxy for the whole population,
the way a city's skyline hints at how many people live inside it. A new
study using the James Webb Space Telescope suggests that method has been
missing most of the picture. Some of the universe's earliest massive
galaxies, it turns out, are carrying three to four times more stellar mass
than previous estimates found, hidden in swarms of small, faint stars that
JWST's predecessors could never resolve
([Penn State](https://www.psu.edu/news/research/story/hidden-stars-suggest-distant-galaxies-are-more-massive-they-appear),
[phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-08-hidden-stars-distant-galaxies-massive.html)).

The study, led by Chloe Cheng, a recent doctoral graduate of Leiden
University, and Mariska Kriek, professor of extragalactic astronomy at
Leiden Observatory, combined JWST's infrared vision with earlier
ground-based observations from the European Southern Observatory's Very
Large Telescope to model nine massive galaxies from the early universe, one
of them dating to less than 1.5 billion years after the Big Bang. It
appears in Nature Astronomy
([DOI: 10.1038/s41550-026-02932-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41550-026-02932-4)).

The trouble with counting stars in a galaxy eight or more billion light-years
away is that only the brightest ones — the hot, massive stars that burn out
fast — are bright enough to see individually. Everything fainter blurs into
an unresolved haze, and astronomers have long assumed that haze follows the
same ratio of bright-to-faint stars observed in nearby, present-day galaxies.
Cheng and her colleagues built models that let that ratio vary and found
that, for these early systems, it does not hold: there are far more small
stars per bright one than the modern universe would predict.

"These galaxies are different — they are more massive than we expected —
like a lot more massive, they have three or four times more mass than we
expected," said Joel Leja, an associate professor of astronomy and
astrophysics at Penn State and a co-author on the study. Cheng described the
effect in more visual terms: "Our models demonstrate that a far more
numerous population of low-mass stars is concealed by those rare, bright
stars, like houses hidden between skyscrapers."

The gap matters beyond bookkeeping. Galaxy formation models calibrate how
quickly matter collapses into stars, how galaxies grow and merge, and how
soon a universe assembling itself out of hydrogen and helium could produce
something as massive as what JWST is now finding at very early cosmic times.
If those early galaxies were quietly three to four times heavier than
believed, the timeline for how fast structure formed in the young universe
needs revising upward, not because something exotic happened, but because
the accounting was incomplete.

There is a gentler implication too. Mariska Kriek, the study's senior
author, pointed out that an abundance of low-mass stars means an abundance
of the kind of star our own sun is — the kind most likely to host planets.
"As many planets orbit low-mass stars, this could indicate more planets
formed in the early universe," she said. A universe that looked sparse and
extreme in its first couple of billion years may instead have been quietly
building the ordinary architecture — small stars, small planets — that the
rest of cosmic history would run on. It is a reminder that some of the
biggest revisions in astronomy come not from spotting something new and
strange, but from noticing how much was there all along, simply too dim to
count.
