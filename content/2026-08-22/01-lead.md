Every inertial-fusion reactor design has the same quiet bottleneck: before a
laser can compress a fuel pellet, someone has to grow a near-perfect layer of
frozen hydrogen fuel inside a shell smaller than a peppercorn, smooth to
within a whisker of ideal, or the implosion falls apart. At the National
Ignition Facility, that crystal-growing step has taken the better part of a
week. Inertia Enterprises, a Livermore, California fusion startup, announced
this week that it has cut that same process down to two to three hours, with
a credible path to under one
([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/20/inertia-enterprises-finds-a-way-to-make-its-fusion-fuel-fast/)).

The fuel pellets in question are tiny diamond spheres holding a frozen shell
of deuterium and tritium around a core of the same gas. Getting that frozen
shell to freeze evenly, in a smooth single layer, is one of the harder
manufacturing problems in the whole fusion pipeline, and it has to happen
over and over for a power plant that needs about ten pellets ignited every
second. NIF's own layering process, refined over two decades to hit the
tolerances behind 2022's ignition breakthrough, simply was not built for
that kind of throughput.

Inertia's fix, developed with Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory under a
public-private partnership, condensed the crystal-growth phase itself to
roughly 30 minutes and verified the results against the same LLNL codes used
to design the original ignition shot, so the faster layers are being checked
against the same physics standard rather than a looser one
([The Fusion Report](https://thefusionreport.substack.com/p/this-weeks-fusion-news-august-21)).
The company says the results stay well within ignition-proven tolerances.
That matters because Inertia's planned reactor uses a 10-megajoule laser,
five times more powerful than NIF's 2-megajoule system, which makes its
implosions considerably more forgiving of small imperfections in the ice
layer — the faster manufacturing, in other words, is not a corner cut, but
a corner that no longer needs to be turned so carefully.

The practical payoff compounds. Faster fuel manufacturing means smaller
factories, since a plant no longer needs days of pellets queued up in cold
storage to keep a reactor fed. It also eases one of fusion's least
glamorous but most real constraints: tritium is scarce and expensive, with
only around 25 kilograms held in stockpiles worldwide and a market price
near $30,000 a gram, so any process that shortens the time fuel spends
sitting around, or reduces how much needs to be on hand at once, is a
meaningful lever on cost. Inertia co-founder and CEO Jeff Lawson summed up
the shape of the win simply: the facility gets smaller, the whole process
gets faster, and the system gets more efficient throughout.

This is the first of ten milestones Inertia has laid out on its path to a
commercial demonstration plant, and it is the kind of progress that rarely
makes headlines on its own — a manufacturing step, measured in hours instead
of days. But fusion's road to commercial power has always run as much
through problems like this one as through the ignition physics that gets
the attention. Solve enough of them, quietly, and a power plant stops being
a physics experiment and starts being a factory.
