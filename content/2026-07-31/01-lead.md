When heavy rain triggered landslides across Japan's Noto Peninsula in
September 2024, months after a major earthquake had already battered the
region, disaster responders needed to know quickly where the ground had
failed. Optical satellites were no help — the same storm clouds blocking
rescue helicopters were blocking the cameras. What worked was synthetic
aperture radar: satellites that build images from reflected radio waves
instead of light, and so can see straight through cloud cover and
darkness alike. The images came from QPS-SAR, a constellation built by a
small Japanese company called iQPS, and they let responders compare
before-and-after data to map the damage almost as fast as it happened.

On July 30, Rocket Lab announced it would launch three more of those
satellites. It's the third dedicated multi-launch deal the two companies
have signed in under a year, bringing the total number of Electron
missions booked for iQPS to 18 — a remarkable figure for a company that
didn't exist twenty years ago, founded in 2005 by emeritus professors at
Kyushu University with the modest ambition of building a space industry
in Kyushu. The new missions will fly from Rocket Lab's launch complex in
New Zealand starting in late 2027, each carrying a single QPS-SAR
satellite to a 575-kilometer orbit, and each fitted with Rocket Lab's own
Motorized Lightband separation hardware to keep the schedule tight
([Rocket Lab](https://rocketlabcorp.com/updates/rocket-lab-secures-multi-launch-deal-with-iqps-for-three-dedicated-missions/);
[Investing News Network](https://investingnews.com/rocket-lab-secures-multi-launch-deal-with-iqps-for-three-dedicated-missions/)).

The deal lands not long after a launch abort in June left iQPS a
satellite behind schedule, its next radar craft still waiting for a slot
no earlier than August — a reminder of how thin the margin still is in
small-satellite launch, and how much a company like iQPS depends on a
launch partner it can trust to show up again and again
([TechTimes](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/322286/20260730/rocket-lab-books-flight-18-iqps-after-june-abort-stalled-japans-radar-fleet.htm)).
That trust has a track record behind it: Electron has delivered seven
QPS-SAR satellites to orbit since 2023 with a perfect mission-success
record, which is precisely the kind of unglamorous reliability that lets
a disaster-response agency build its planning around a satellite that
hasn't launched yet.

iQPS is aiming for a 24-satellite constellation by 2028 and 36 by 2030,
enough radar coverage to revisit almost any point on Earth roughly every
ten minutes — day or night, clear skies or typhoon. That's a genuinely
new capability for a country that sits on top of some of the world's
most active fault lines and gets hit by some of its heaviest rains. None
of this is glamorous the way a crewed launch or a Mars mission is. It's
a small rocket company and a small satellite company, quietly building
out the infrastructure that will matter most on the worst day of
someone's year — and doing it three contracts running, which is its own
kind of vote of confidence.
