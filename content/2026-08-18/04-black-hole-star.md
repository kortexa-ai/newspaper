title: Astronomers think they've solved the mystery of the universe's "little red dots"
kicker: Space
author: The Editors
summary: A James Webb Space Telescope survey found a black hole wrapped in a dense cocoon of hydrogen, glowing like a star 100 billion times over — and it may explain a puzzle that has shown up in nearly every deep-field image Webb has taken.
order: 4
---

Since it started returning images, the James Webb Space Telescope has kept
turning up small, intensely red dots scattered across its deepest fields of
the early universe — too numerous and too strange to explain with ordinary
galaxies. A team led by Rohan Naidu, a NASA Hubble Fellow at MIT's Kavli
Institute for Astrophysics and Space Research, thinks it has found what one
of them actually is. While hunting for the universe's earliest galaxies
under a survey called Mirage or Miracle, the team spotted an object, dated
to about 660 million years after the Big Bang, that outshines any known star
by a factor of roughly 100 billion
([MIT News](https://news.mit.edu/2026/astronomers-discover-brand-new-type-astrophysical-object-black-hole-star-0812)).

The object, catalogued as MoM-BH*-1, isn't a star in the usual sense. Naidu's
team argues it's a black hole about 100,000 times the mass of the Sun,
wrapped in a hydrogen envelope roughly the size of the solar system so dense
that the whole structure radiates like a star's surface rather than a black
hole's accretion disk. "We think there is a central black hole that is
100,000 times as massive as the sun," Naidu said. "And around this black
hole, there would be this very extended envelope of gas that looks like a
star the size of the solar system." Co-author Robert Simcoe, director of
MIT's Kavli Institute, put the underlying question more simply: "Could you
make something that red using just hydrogen, without any dust? To our
surprise, it turns out you can, if you have an extremely dense screen of
hydrogen." The team says the spectral signature ruled out ordinary stars
entirely — "the deepest break we have ever observed in any object," in
Naidu's words. If black hole stars like this one turn out to be common,
they'd resolve a years-old puzzle in one stroke: not a new kind of galaxy,
but an early, self-disguising stage in how the universe's largest black
holes got their start.
