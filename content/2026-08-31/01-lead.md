Nearly five years after Congress created the Broadband Equity, Access and
Deployment program with a one-time $42.45 billion appropriation, the last
outstanding piece fell into place this week. The National
Telecommunications and Information Administration announced on August 26
that it had approved Illinois's final proposal, completing the review of
all 56 state and territorial BEAD plans
([Wireless Estimator](https://wirelessestimator.com/articles/2026/illinois-becomes-the-final-domino-as-ntia-approves-all-56-bead-plans/)).
"BEAD's core mission is to ensure universal broadband availability across
our country," NTIA Administrator Arielle Roth said of the milestone.

Illinois had been the lone holdout for roughly five weeks, ever since
California's plan cleared review on July 20. NTIA had sent Illinois's
submission back for revisions to meet the program's efficiency standards
before finally signing off
([Wireless Estimator](https://wirelessestimator.com/articles/2026/illinois-becomes-the-final-domino-as-ntia-approves-all-56-bead-plans/)).
Three days later, on August 29, Governor JB Pritzker's office put a number
on what that approval unlocks for the state: $1.04 billion, aimed at
roughly 350,000 residents in homes, businesses and community institutions
that still lack a reliable connection
([WHIG](https://whig.com/2026/08/29/illinois-approved-for-1-billion-in-federal-broadband-funding/)).
Most of that money will run fiber — 68 percent of the plan, with fixed
wireless, satellite and cable covering the rest — to the parts of the
state a market built on density has never found worth wiring.

"Communities all across Illinois will finally be able to access the
high-quality internet they need to thrive in the digital age," Pritzker
said. Senator Dick Durbin called it "a historic investment in closing the
digital divide, putting internet access within reach for thousands"
([WHIG](https://whig.com/2026/08/29/illinois-approved-for-1-billion-in-federal-broadband-funding/)).
The state's existing Connect Illinois program, a $420 million predecessor
effort, has already connected more than 20,600 households — a preview of
the scale the new federal money is meant to multiply.

BEAD itself has had a long, uneven road to this point. The program was
funded in 2021, and its rules were rewritten by the current administration
in mid-2025 under a "Benefit of the Bargain" framework that sent every
state back through a new competitive-bidding round — a process that
delayed shovels going into the ground but, NTIA says, saved taxpayers
roughly $21 billion along the way
([Wireless Estimator](https://wirelessestimator.com/articles/2026/illinois-becomes-the-final-domino-as-ntia-approves-all-56-bead-plans/)).
Nationally, the program was built around closing the gap for the
roughly 7 million American homes and businesses still without high-speed
service.

None of this means fiber reaches anyone's house tomorrow. Approval starts
the subgrantee process — commercial providers, local governments and
nonprofits in each state now bid for the money and begin construction,
with most states targeting substantial completion by 2031. But for a
program that has spent years as a list of states still waiting, the list
is, for the first time, empty. Every state and territory in the country
now has a cleared plan and a funded path to finishing the job.
