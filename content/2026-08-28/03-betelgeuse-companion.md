---
title: Astronomers finally get a clear look at Betelgeuse's hidden companion
kicker: Science
author: The Editors
summary: A century-old suspicion about the famous red supergiant is now a picture, taken with ESO's Very Large Telescope and published this week in Astronomy & Astrophysics.
order: 3
---

For nearly a hundred years, astronomers have suspected that Betelgeuse —
the bright red supergiant marking Orion's shoulder — doesn't shine alone.
Subtle irregularities in its brightness hinted at an unseen companion, but
actually seeing a faint stellar partner this close to one of the sky's
brightest stars is like trying to photograph a match struck next to a
searchlight. This week, a team led by Miguel Montargès of the Observatoire
de Paris - PSL published the clearest evidence yet that the companion is
real, in a paper titled "VLT/SPHERE images the candidate companion of
Betelgeuse" in Astronomy & Astrophysics
([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/08/260827010514.htm)).

The image was captured in December 2024 using the SPHERE instrument on
the European Southern Observatory's Very Large Telescope in Chile, timed
to catch the companion — now provisionally named Betelgeuse B — at the
point in its orbit where it sits furthest from its overwhelming neighbor
as seen from Earth
([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/08/260827010514.htm)).
That timing, plus careful processing to suppress Betelgeuse's glare, is
what finally pulled a clean signal out of the noise.

The picture also revised the story. Betelgeuse B had been expected to
weigh in at roughly the mass of the Sun; the new observations put it
closer to two or three solar masses, which helps explain why it was
detectable at all — a heavier young star burns brighter than a lightweight
one. "I jumped from my chair when I saw the processed images," Montargès
said of the result
([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/08/260827010514.htm)).

It's a reminder that some of the best-studied objects in the sky still
have surprises in them, and that patience — waiting for the right night,
the right orbital alignment, the right processing technique — is still
how a lot of astronomy gets done.
