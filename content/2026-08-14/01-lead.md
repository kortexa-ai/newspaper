Most infrastructure projects measure setbacks in delays and cost overruns.
Revolution Wind measured its setbacks in stop-work orders, and it survived
two of them. On August 13, Ørsted's chief executive, Rasmus Errboe, told
investors the offshore wind farm off Rhode Island is more than 95 percent
complete, with 61 of its 65 turbines installed and the rest on track to go
up "over the coming months," putting the project on a path to full
commercial operation by the end of the year
([CT Mirror](https://ctmirror.org/2026/08/13/revolution-wind-completed-2026-orsted/);
[Hartford Business Journal](https://hartfordbusiness.com/article/revolution-wind-project-on-track-for-completion-this-year-developer-says/)).

The project had been 80 percent finished, with 45 turbines standing, when
the Trump administration's Bureau of Ocean Energy Management ordered work
stopped on August 22, 2025. A federal judge reinstated construction the
following month, finding that the government "failed to make any factual
findings or cite any reasons" for the halt. That should have settled it.
Instead, on December 22, 2025, the administration issued a second
suspension order, this one citing national security concerns about
offshore radar and affecting five East Coast wind projects at once, with
just seven of Revolution Wind's turbines left to install. A federal court
in Washington granted a preliminary injunction in January 2026, and
construction resumed for good
([Connecticut Public](https://www.ctpublic.org/news/2026-03-13/revolution-wind-comes-online-new-england-power-grid)).

Two months later, on March 13, 2026, Revolution Wind sent its first
electricity to the New England grid. Connecticut Governor Ned Lamont
called it key to "diversifying our energy supply and lowering utility
costs for families and businesses," while Rhode Island Governor Dan McKee
called it "an important step forward for Rhode Island's energy future"
([Connecticut Public](https://www.ctpublic.org/news/2026-03-13/revolution-wind-comes-online-new-england-power-grid);
[Rhode Island Current](https://rhodeislandcurrent.com/2026/03/16/weve-got-the-power-revolution-wind-begins-delivering-electricity-to-regional-grid/)).

When finished, Revolution Wind will generate up to 704 megawatts — enough
for roughly 350,000 homes across Connecticut and Rhode Island, and about
2.5 percent of all the electricity New England uses
([Connecticut Public](https://www.ctpublic.org/news/2026-03-13/revolution-wind-comes-online-new-england-power-grid)).
It is the first offshore wind project under contract to supply power to
Connecticut customers, built by a Danish company, financed by
institutional investors, and now nearly complete despite a federal
government that tried, twice, to stop it before it could finish.

None of that makes Revolution Wind unusual as a piece of engineering — 65
turbines standing in the Atlantic is by now a familiar sight up and down
the East Coast. What makes it worth a second look is what it says about
how large infrastructure gets built in a contested political climate: not
by avoiding the fight, but by outlasting it, one court ruling and one
turbine at a time. Four turbines remain. Barring a third attempt to stop
them, they'll be turning by the end of the year.
