---
title: A cooler way to grow graphene, straight from waste carbon
kicker: Science
author: The Editors
summary: Researchers in Japan and the UK grew graphene at 300°C instead of the usual 900°C, and used the lower heat to steer what form it takes.
order: 3
---

Making graphene has always meant turning up the heat — typically to around
900°C, hot enough to limit both the energy efficiency of the process and
the control researchers have over the material it produces. A team from
Tohoku University and Queen Mary University of London has found a way
around that, growing graphene-based materials at temperatures as low as
300°C, according to a study published in the *Journal of the American
Chemical Society*
([Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-08-temperature-graphene-growth-route-sustainable.html)).

The trick was swapping the usual carbon feedstock for acetylene gas, run
over a cerium oxide surface that turns out to be unusually good at
encouraging carbon atoms to link up into graphene's signature
honeycomb lattice even at low heat. "By switching to highly reactive
acetylene and pairing it with cerium oxide, we established a low-temperature
growth process," said Tohoku University associate professor Takeharu
Yoshii
([Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-08-temperature-graphene-growth-route-sustainable.html)).

Temperature, it turns out, is also a dial. At 300°C, the process yields
blue-fluorescing graphene quantum dots; push it to 450°C and it produces
aggregated graphene; at 600°C, a high-surface-area porous form emerges —
each a different material for a different job, from displays to catalysis
to energy storage, all from the same low-heat setup
([Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-08-temperature-graphene-growth-route-sustainable.html)).

The lower temperature opens a door beyond energy savings, too: it makes it
plausible to build graphene out of carbon that would otherwise go to
waste. "Instead of using surplus hydrocarbons or low-grade carbon
resources as fuel, we can now aim to 'upgrade' them," said professor
Hirotomo Nishihara, pointing to industrial waste gases, biomass and
recycled plastics as potential feedstocks
([Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-08-temperature-graphene-growth-route-sustainable.html)).
A material once defined by how much heat it demanded may end up defined,
instead, by what it can be made from.
