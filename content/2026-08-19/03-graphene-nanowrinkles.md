---
title: A sharper wrinkle in graphene turns out to be a tiny power source
kicker: Materials
author: The Editors
summary: Rice University researchers found that sharply curved wrinkles in graphene generate far stronger electrical polarization than theory predicted, opening a path to tuning electronics by reshaping them rather than re-engineering their chemistry.
order: 3
---

A wrinkle in a sheet of graphene, it turns out, isn't just a cosmetic
flaw — it can behave like a tiny electrical generator. Researchers at Rice
University, publishing in *Advanced Materials*, found that tightly curved
nanowrinkles in graphene produce electrical charge separation between
100,000 and 10 million times stronger than similar effects seen in
much larger materials, providing some of the clearest experimental evidence
yet for flexoelectricity in an atomically thin material
([Rice News](https://news.rice.edu/news/2026/rice-researchers-show-graphene-nanowrinkles-can-reshape-electricity);
[Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-08-graphene-nanowrinkles-reshape-electricity-future.html);
[EurekAlert!](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1139927)).

Flexoelectricity describes how uneven bending in a material can generate an
electric charge, distinct from the piezoelectric effect that shows up under
uniform strain. The Rice team found that what mattered most wasn't how big
a wrinkle was, but how sharply it curved. "The sharpness of the wrinkle
turned out to be much more important than its overall size," said Sathvik
Iyengar, one of the researchers. The measurements gave physicist Vincent
Meunier's team an unexpected connection to theoretical predictions his
group had made years earlier: "We realized that the unusual signals could
provide an experimental connection to an idea we had predicted many years
earlier," he said. Rice's Pulickel Ajayan summed up the appeal of the
result: "Our work shows that even an ordinary wrinkle can become an
extraordinary electronic feature when viewed at the atomic scale."

The practical upshot is a new dial for engineers to turn. Instead of
changing what a material is made of to tune its electrical behavior,
researchers could instead just reshape it — folding, wrinkling, or
otherwise deforming atomically thin sheets to get the electrical response
they want. That could eventually feed into more sensitive sensors and
thinner, more efficient electronics, built not from new chemistry but from
old graphene bent into cleverer shapes.
