---
title: Physicists switched off a superconductor to learn how it turns on
kicker: Science
author: The Editors
summary: By screening the electric interactions inside magic-angle graphene, a Manchester-led team completely suppressed its superconductivity — strong evidence for what actually causes it.
order: 2
---

Magic-angle graphene — two sheets of carbon atoms twisted against each
other by a precise 1.1 degrees — has puzzled physicists since 2018, when it
turned out to superconduct despite having none of the ingredients theory
said superconductors needed. Two explanations have competed ever since:
electrons pairing up by nudging the material's atomic lattice, the
conventional route, or electrons pairing directly with each other. A team
led by Julien Barrier at the University of Manchester's National Graphene
Institute has now built a device precise enough to test the difference,
and the electrons appear to have won
([Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-09-magic-angle-graphene-evidence-unconventional.html);
[Physical Review X](https://journals.aps.org/prx/abstract/10.1103/z9qg-287y)).

The trick was screening. The researchers placed a second twisted graphene
bilayer less than a nanometer away — close enough to interact electrically,
but electronically isolated — and tuned how many charge carriers sat in
it. That progressively weakened the Coulomb repulsion between electrons in
the magic-angle layer, the very interaction an "unconventional" theory
would require. As the screening increased, the superconductivity weakened
in step, and eventually vanished. "When we switched on the screening,
superconductivity was completely suppressed, providing clear experimental
evidence it originates from electron interactions," said co-author Alexey
Berdyugin, now at the National University of Singapore.

It's a rare case of an experiment cleanly ruling something out: if lattice
vibrations were doing the real work, screening the electrons' mutual
repulsion shouldn't have touched the effect, but it killed it outright. As
Nobel laureate and co-author Andre Geim put it, the stakes reach beyond one
material: "Unless we understand what makes superconductivity work, we are
unlikely to reach room-temperature superconductivity or commercial
utility." Magic-angle graphene itself only superconducts near absolute
zero, but a clean answer to why narrows the search for materials that
might do the same job at temperatures worth caring about.
