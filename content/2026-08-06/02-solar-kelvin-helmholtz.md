Physicists have long predicted that the Sun's surface should
occasionally do something familiar from Earth's own atmosphere: when
two layers of fluid slide past each other at different speeds, the
friction between them rolls up into spiraling vortices, the same
instability that carves wave patterns into clouds and ocean currents.
It's called a Kelvin-Helmholtz instability, and until this week,
nobody had ever actually seen it happen on the Sun.

An international team from the National Solar Observatory, NSF NCAR's
High Altitude Observatory, and Germany's Max Planck Institute for
Solar System Research found the telltale signature — tiny, swirling
whirlpool patterns — in the sharpest images ever taken of the solar
surface, captured by the NSF's Daniel K. Inouye Solar Telescope on
Maui. The telescope's 13-foot mirror, the largest of any solar
telescope in the world, gathers roughly seven times more light than
its predecessors, resolving detail on the photosphere that simply
wasn't visible before. The team published its findings August 5 in
*Nature*
([Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10871-3);
[NSO](https://nso.edu/press-release/nsf-inouye-solar-telescope-enables-major-discovery-of-a-hidden-solar-process/);
[CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/08/05/science/inouye-telescope-sun-images-magnetic-energy)).

The discovery matters beyond the satisfaction of confirming a
decades-old prediction. One of the Sun's stubborn mysteries is why
its outer atmosphere, the corona, burns millions of degrees hotter
than the surface directly beneath it — the equivalent of the air a
few feet above a stove being far hotter than the burner itself.
Researchers suspect these newly observed instabilities help churn and
mix magnetic energy at the surface, energy that could later erupt
outward and help account for that coronal heating, and for the solar
flares and mass ejections that occasionally send charged particles
toward Earth, disrupting satellites and power grids.

It's a first observation, not a finished theory — years of follow-up
work will be needed to establish exactly how much of the corona's
heat and the Sun's unpredictable flare activity this mechanism
actually explains. But having direct images of a process that
theorists could previously only model gives solar physicists a real
foothold on a question that shapes how well we can forecast space
weather here on Earth.
