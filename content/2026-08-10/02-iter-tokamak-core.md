title: ITER's fusion reactor core is now two-thirds built, months ahead of plan
kicker: Climate
author: The Editors
summary: A 1,100-tonne sector of the world's largest fusion experiment was lifted into its pit in France in a 30-hour operation, putting the ITER tokamak six months ahead of its assembly schedule.

---

Building the world's largest tokamak means periodically lowering
thousand-tonne pieces of it into a pit with almost no room for error. ITER,
the international fusion megaproject under construction in southern France,
completed its sixth such lift on July 28: an approximately 1,100-tonne
sector module, suspended with its lifting rig at nearly 1,400 tonnes,
lowered into the machine's torus-shaped core over a 30-hour operation
involving more than 100 staff and contractors. With it, two-thirds of the
tokamak's nine sector modules are now in place
([ITER](https://www.iter.org/two-thirds-iter-tokamak-core-now-place)).

The installed sector is built around a vacuum vessel component manufactured
in the Republic of Korea, one of the seven ITER members — along with China,
the European Union, India, Japan, Russia, and the United States — each
building pieces of the reactor in parallel before they converge on the
assembly site. ITER's teams have been lowering these modules into place
since April 2025, and each lift has gone a little faster and more smoothly
than the last as the crews refine the choreography. The ninth and final
sector is now scheduled for installation in 2027 — and the project reports
it is running almost six months ahead of its assembly schedule
([World Nuclear News](https://world-nuclear-news.org/articles/in-pictures-iter-tokamak-core-assembly-progressing)).

ITER won't produce electricity; it's an experiment designed to prove that a
fusion reaction can sustain itself and produce more energy than it consumes,
clearing the physics and engineering path for the fusion power plants that
would follow. Megaprojects like this one are more often news for slipping
timelines than beating them, which makes an assembly milestone landing
early worth noting on its own. Two-thirds of a machine built to recreate
conditions found naturally only inside stars is now sitting, bolted and
aligned, in a pit in Provence.
