---
title: NASA's quiet supersonic jet passes flight 25, and the real test begins
kicker: Infrastructure
author: The Editors
summary: The X-59 hit Mach 1.2 over the Mojave in its 25th test flight, and NASA says the program is now moving into the acoustic tests that will decide whether its shape really turns a sonic boom into a quiet thump.
order: 3
---

NASA's X-59 is built around a single bet: that a long, needle-nosed
airframe can spread out the shockwaves of supersonic flight so that,
instead of a boom, people on the ground hear a soft thump. In an update
posted this week, the agency recapped the aircraft's 25th test flight — a
72-minute flight out of Armstrong Flight Research Center in Edwards,
California, reaching Mach 1.2 at roughly 49,000 feet — and said the
program is now shifting into the phase that actually tests that bet
([NASA Quesst
blog](https://www.nasa.gov/blogs/quesst/2026/09/04/nasas-x-59-aircraft-builds-momentum-through-25th-flight/)).

Each flight so far has been about proving the aircraft flies as designed.
"Each test point has validated our models and predictions, and it has
strengthened our confidence in the aircraft's performance," said project
manager Cathy Bahm. The next phase, acoustic validation, points the
instruments outward instead: ground- and air-based sensors will measure
what the X-59's shockwave actually sounds like as it passes overhead.
"Building and flying a brand-new aircraft is an extraordinary
accomplishment, but the next phase is where the real research begins,"
said Larry Cliatt, the mission's acoustic validation technical lead.

That research is the whole point of Quesst. The United States has banned
civilian overland supersonic flight since 1973, largely because of the
boom. If the X-59's acoustic data holds up, NASA plans to hand it to
regulators as the evidence needed to consider a noise-based standard
instead of an outright ban — the first real opening for supersonic travel
over land in half a century.
