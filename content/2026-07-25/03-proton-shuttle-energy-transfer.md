---
title: A proton that shuttles back and forth just sped up solar cell chemistry
kicker: Materials
author: The Editors
summary: Researchers found that a proton briefly stepping out of place and returning can dramatically speed how energy moves between quantum dots and nearby molecules — a mechanism that could sharpen solar cells, lasers, and catalysts.
order: 3
---

Some of the most useful energy in a solar cell or a catalyst spends
its life in a form physicists call a triplet state — a particular spin
arrangement that's excellent at storing energy but notoriously slow
and inefficient to move between materials. Getting that energy from,
say, a light-absorbing quantum dot to a neighboring molecule that can
put it to work has long been one of the fiddlier problems in
materials chemistry.

A team studying zinc selenide quantum dots found an unexpected helper:
a proton that shuttles. In their system, the quantum dots are anchored
to phenol-pyridine molecules on their surface. When light excites the
quantum dot, a proton briefly hops from the phenol to the pyridine and
then back to where it started — and that small round trip turns out to
choreograph the whole handoff, coupling a hole transfer and then an
electron transfer so that the triplet energy migrates cleanly from the
quantum dot to the acceptor molecule. The mechanism, described as
proton-shuttle-assisted triplet energy transfer, was published in
*Nature Materials*
([DOI: 10.1038/s41563-026-02535-4](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41563-026-02535-4);
reported by [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/07/260722032130.htm)
and [SciTechDaily](https://scitechdaily.com/new-quantum-mechanism-boosts-energy-transfer-in-nanomaterials/),
based on a release from
[EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1120559)).

It's a small mechanism with a large list of places it might matter.
Triplet energy transfer of exactly this kind underlies how well solar
cells convert light, how efficiently certain lasers operate, and how
readily some catalytic reactions proceed. A proton that can be
engineered to shuttle on cue gives chemists a new lever: a way to tune
how fast and how completely that energy moves, rather than simply
hoping the materials involved happen to be compatible. There's no
product built on this yet — it's a mechanism paper, not a device — but
mechanism papers are how the next decade's devices get designed. Every
solar cell and laser that eventually uses this trick will trace back
to a proton that learned to step out and step back.
