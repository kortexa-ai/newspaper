Big batteries usually mean big substations. Base Power is betting on
the opposite: small batteries, in ordinary backyards, thousands of
them, adding up to something a grid operator can actually dispatch.
On August 3 the Austin-based startup announced a $1 billion Series D,
less than a year after its last billion-dollar round, valuing the
company at $13 billion
([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/03/base-power-raises-another-1b-to-save-the-grid-using-backyard-batteries/)).
The round was led by Ribbit, Addition, Valor Equity Partners, and
JPMorganChase's Strategic Investment Group, with a16z, Lightspeed, and
Coatue among the other participants.

The mechanics are what make it interesting. Base doesn't sell
batteries — it installs and owns them, on a subscription: customers
pay an installation fee, a monthly charge, and a per-kilowatt-hour
rate, with no large upfront cost. In exchange, Base can draw on that
distributed fleet to sell power back to the grid during the hours
demand spikes, the same hours utilities would otherwise fire up their
most expensive and dirtiest peaker plants. The company, operating in
Texas and Illinois, says it's now installing roughly 100 systems a
day — about 8 megawatt-hours of new storage daily — and aims to double
that pace by the end of the year. Its newest unit, the Base Core, is
built at its Austin facility and holds 39.2 kilowatt-hours, notably
larger than most residential competitors.

It's a virtual power plant, built one backyard at a time, and its case
rests on a simple bet: that a battery a utility can call on during a
heat-wave peak is worth more, to everyone, than a battery that just
sits there backing up a single house.
