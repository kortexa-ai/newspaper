Battery-materials startup Anthro Energy broke ground on August 19 in
Louisville, Kentucky, on a factory the company says is the first US-owned
and operated facility built to mass-produce an advanced electrolyte for
next-generation batteries
([PRWeb](https://www.prweb.com/releases/anthro-energy-breaks-ground-on-americas-first-us-owned-advanced-electrolyte-manufacturing-facility-in-kentucky-302854915.html);
[TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/18/anthro-energy-breaks-ground-on-factory-that-could-pave-the-road-to-solid-state-batteries/)).

Most lithium-ion batteries today rely on a liquid electrolyte to shuttle
ions between electrodes — effective, but flammable, and a big part of why
battery fires make headlines. Anthro's material, branded Proteus, is a
polymer that flows like a liquid during manufacturing, so it can be
injected into a cell the way conventional electrolyte is, and then
solidifies. CEO David Mackanic says the result is a battery "10 to 15 times
stronger" mechanically than one built with a liquid electrolyte, with the
flammability risk designed out. Once running, the Louisville plant is
expected to produce roughly 12,000 metric tons of the material a year —
enough to supply about 25 gigawatt-hours of battery production, or upwards
of 300,000 electric vehicles' worth of cells — with production targeted for
late 2027.

The project is a rare case of federal and state incentives lining up behind
a single domestic supply chain: $24.9 million from the Department of Energy
under the bipartisan infrastructure law, $18.4 million in investment tax
credits through the Inflation Reduction Act's 48C program for advanced
energy projects, and $2.3 million in Kentucky state incentives, on top of
Anthro's own investment. The buildout is expected to support around 390
construction jobs, settling into 110 permanent manufacturing and technical
positions once the plant is running. "Today's groundbreaking represents
much more than the start of construction," Mackanic said at the event; "it
marks the beginning of a new chapter for American battery manufacturing." A
domestic, non-Chinese source for a key battery input is exactly the kind of
unglamorous infrastructure that determines whether the next decade of
electric vehicles and grid storage gets built on a supply chain this
country actually controls.
