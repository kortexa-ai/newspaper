---
title: A new kind of cosmic object may explain the universe's mysterious "little red dots"
kicker: Science
author: The Editors
summary: Webb telescope data revealed a black hole wrapped in a glowing cocoon of gas just 660 million years after the Big Bang, giving astronomers their clearest look yet at how the earliest supermassive black holes grew so fast.
order: 3
---

For a few years now, the James Webb Space Telescope has been finding
faint, compact smudges scattered across images of the early universe —
"little red dots" that didn't fit any known category of star, galaxy, or
black hole. A team led by Rohan Naidu at the University of Hawai'i's
Institute for Astronomy and Jorryt Matthee at the Institute of Science
and Technology Austria may have finally identified what one of them is:
a newly forming black hole, roughly 100,000 times the sun's mass,
wrapped in a dense cocoon of hydrogen gas so thick it behaves almost like
the atmosphere of a star. They're calling the object a "black hole star,"
and this one — designated MoM-BH*-1 — is seen as it existed just 660
million years after the Big Bang, its light having traveled 13 billion
years to reach Webb's mirrors
([Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10846-4); [Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-08-mirage-miracle-jwst-earliest-black.html)).

The evidence is in the light itself. MoM-BH*-1 shows one of the steepest
drops in brightness ever recorded across what astronomers call the
Balmer break — nearly eight times dimmer on one side of a specific
wavelength than the other, versus roughly two and a half times for an
ordinary star like Vega. That kind of drop is what you'd expect from a
thick shroud of gas absorbing and re-emitting a black hole's light,
rather than from starlight alone. "The spectrum we detected is our best
evidence of a cloak of gas feeding an early-forming black hole," Naidu
said ([Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-08-mirage-miracle-jwst-earliest-black.html)).

If black hole stars turn out to be common among the little red dots, they
would solve two puzzles at once: what these strange objects actually are,
and how the universe managed to grow supermassive black holes so early,
so fast. A shroud of infalling gas, it turns out, may be exactly the kind
of feeding mechanism that lets a black hole put on weight before a galaxy
has even finished forming around it.
