Some infrastructure gets built in a single dramatic gesture. Most of it gets built the slow way — piece by piece, launch by launch, over years, until one day the last piece goes up and a plan that started as a PowerPoint slide is quietly, completely real. On Saturday, September 13, SES reached that day.

A SpaceX Falcon 9 lifted off from Space Launch Complex 40 at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station at 2:49 p.m. EDT, carrying the eleventh, twelfth and thirteenth satellites of the O3b mPOWER constellation — Boeing-built spacecraft, each with a dry mass of about 1,900 kilograms, deployed in a staggered sequence roughly 34 minutes after liftoff, seven minutes apart ([Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/2026/09/13/live-coverage-spacex-to-launch-final-3-o3b-mpower-satellites-for-ses/)). With that, the constellation SES first began assembling in December 2022 is complete: 13 spacecraft in medium Earth orbit, roughly 8,000 kilometers up, built on Boeing's software-defined 702X satellite bus.

It wasn't a straight line getting here. An electrical issue turned up in the fifth and sixth satellites partway through the campaign, prompting Boeing to revise the design for later units and SES to add two extra spacecraft to the constellation as insurance — the kind of mid-flight correction that rarely makes headlines but is exactly the sort of unglamorous engineering discipline that keeps a program from becoming a cautionary tale instead of a success story. "The completion of the O3b mPOWER system reinforces our proven leadership in MEO," said Xavier Bertran, SES's chief product officer. Boeing Satellite Systems president Ryan Reid credited the constellation's "software-defined payload technologies" with meeting the needs of what he called "demanding missions of commercial, joint-force and allied military customers."

Medium Earth orbit is the constellation's whole pitch: closer to the planet than the geostationary satellites that have carried much of the world's broadband and broadcast traffic for decades, which cuts the round-trip delay that makes geostationary links feel sluggish for anything interactive, while still needing far fewer spacecraft than a low-Earth-orbit mega-constellation to cover the globe. O3b mPOWER's satellites are electronically steerable, able to redirect their throughput — anywhere from tens of megabits per second up to multiple gigabits — to wherever demand shows up next, whether that's a cruise ship crossing an ocean, an airliner over a data-poor stretch of ocean, a government outpost, or a community that has simply never had a reliable connection before.

The ten satellites already on orbit have been carrying live traffic; these last three still have to raise their orbits and pass a full checkout before they officially join service, which SES expects by mid-2027. In the meantime, the launch itself made a little history of its own: the 700th Falcon rocket SpaceX has flown, 687 Falcon 9s and 13 Falcon Heavys combined, and the 400th orbital launch overall from SLC-40. The first-stage booster, on its 29th flight, landed on the droneship *A Shortfall of Gravitas* eight and a half minutes after liftoff — the 661st successful Falcon booster landing, a milestone so routine by now that it barely gets a mention, which is itself a small marvel of the last decade in spaceflight.

There is no single dramatic image that captures a constellation completing — no burst of flame, no "we have liftoff" moment. Just thirteen spacecraft, quietly in place, ready to make the next few years of connectivity a little less dependent on where you happen to be standing.
