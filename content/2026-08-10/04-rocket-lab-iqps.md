From Rocket Lab's pad on New Zealand's Mahia Peninsula, an Electron rocket
lifted off the evening of August 6 carrying a single satellite for the
Institute for Q-shu Pioneers of Space (iQPS), a Japanese company building a
constellation of small radar satellites. Named "The Grain Goddess
Provides," the mission delivered QPS-SAR-13 to orbit, the eighth satellite
Rocket Lab has launched for iQPS's synthetic-aperture-radar network, with
100 percent mission success across all eight flights
([Rocket Lab](https://rocketlabcorp.com/updates/rocket-lab-successfully-completes-92nd-electron-mission-8th-launch-for-iqps-earth-imaging-constellation/)).

Synthetic-aperture radar can image the ground through cloud cover and
darkness, which is what makes a constellation of these satellites valuable:
more satellites in more orbital planes means more frequent looks at any
given spot on Earth, useful for everything from disaster response to crop
monitoring. iQPS still has ten more dedicated Electron launches booked to
complete its planned constellation before 2030, and Rocket Lab's Electron
has now flown 92 times overall — its 13th mission of the year alone.

None of this is a single dramatic breakthrough. It's the opposite: a small
launch company and a small satellite operator quietly executing on a
multi-year buildout, one successful flight at a time, until "another
Rocket Lab launch" barely makes news anymore. That kind of boring
reliability is its own kind of progress — it's what a mature, working space
industry is supposed to look like.
