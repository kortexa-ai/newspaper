Intermetallic compounds — ordered alloys like cobalt-aluminum — have
always been a frustrating trade-off for engineers: exceptionally
strong, and exceptionally prone to snapping rather than bending, which
has kept them out of most structural parts despite decades of
interest from the aerospace and energy industries. A team at Purdue
University, led by materials engineering professor Xinghang Zhang with
first author Ke Xu, has now shown a way past that trade-off, by
redesigning the alloy's internal architecture rather than its
chemistry
([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/07/260729051524.htm)).

Using magnetron sputtering — building the material up from vapor
rather than casting it from molten metal — the team engineered a
nanolaminate cobalt-aluminum structure threaded with flexible,
amorphous interfaces and a deliberately high density of dislocations,
the microscopic crystal defects that let metals deform instead of
crack. The result reached a yield strength of 6 gigapascals, roughly
six to ten times that of high-strength structural steel, while still
sustaining 15 percent plastic strain under compression at room
temperature — meaning it bends under serious load instead of failing
outright
([SciTechDaily](https://scitechdaily.com/new-metal-alloy-is-up-to-10-times-stronger-than-structural-steel/)).

"This work provides a new, alternative approach to improve the
plastic deformation capability" of compounds that were previously
written off as too brittle for structural use, Zhang said, noting that
engines built with tougher intermetallics could "spin faster while
sustaining higher centrifugal force." Turbine blades are the most
obvious target — they need to survive exactly the combination of
extreme strength and mechanical give this material now offers — but
the same approach could extend to other brittle intermetallics used in
energy storage, automotive parts, and aerospace hardware.
It's a reminder that some of the most useful materials breakthroughs
aren't new elements or exotic compounds, but old, well-understood ones
rebuilt at a scale small enough to change how they behave.
