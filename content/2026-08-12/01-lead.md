Most satellites that lose their bearings stay lost. LINK, a small servicing
spacecraft built by the startup Katalyst Space Technologies, did not.

LINK launched on July 3 with a specific, unglamorous job: catch up to
NASA's Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory, a gamma-ray-burst hunter that has
watched the sky since 2004, dock with it, and push it into a higher orbit
before atmospheric drag pulls it down for good. It is the kind of mission
that barely makes headlines when it goes right — a small company doing
orbital plumbing so an aging science instrument gets a second act instead
of a fiery reentry.

It did not go right at first. Within weeks of launch, LINK lost most of
its attitude control — the fine thruster and reaction-wheel work that lets
a spacecraft point itself precisely enough to approach and dock with
another object in orbit ([SpaceNews](https://spacenews.com/katalyst-space-makes-progress-in-restoring-control-of-swift-servicing-spacecraft/);
[The Register](https://www.theregister.com/science/2026/08/06/next-step-for-link-spacecraft-a-software-update-that-wont-make-things-worse-are-you-listening-microsoft/5284143)).
For a spacecraft whose entire mission depends on delicate, close-range
maneuvering, that is close to the worst failure short of total loss.

Rather than write LINK off, Katalyst's engineers went to work on the
problem the way software teams solve problems: they rewrote the code. On
August 11, the company uploaded a flight-software update carrying new
attitude-control algorithms designed to hold LINK steady using only the
actuators it had left, after losing two of its three reaction wheels and
part of its thruster capability. NASA's Swift mission team confirmed the
update restored stability, clearing LINK to resume the rendezvous
([NASA Science, Swift mission blog](https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/swift/2026/08/11/nasa-swift-boost-spacecraft-prepares-to-continue-mission/)).

The stakes are real and running on a clock. Swift currently orbits at
about 216 miles (347 kilometers) and is losing altitude. Below roughly 185
miles (300 kilometers), the observatory becomes too hard to safely
maneuver, and the rescue window closes. LINK's revised plan now calls for
a series of burns to capture Swift and boost it back to a stable altitude
before that threshold arrives. NASA's Swift team has separately found ways
to slow the observatory's own decay in the meantime, buying LINK a bit
more runway to complete its approach.

There is something fitting about a spacecraft saving another spacecraft
through nothing more than a firmware update — no new hardware, no second
launch, just a smarter set of instructions for the actuators already on
board. It is also a quiet vote of confidence in the model behind it: that
small, purpose-built servicing companies can show up where a national
space agency can't spare a shuttle mission, take a real hit early in
flight, and still get the job done. Swift has spent two decades cataloging
some of the universe's most violent, fleeting explosions. If LINK's
maneuvers go as planned in the coming weeks, it will get to keep watching.
