Every fusion program chases the same unglamorous number: net facility gain, the
point at which a plant generates more energy than it takes to run the entire
building, not just the reactor core. On Tuesday, Pacific Fusion broke ground on
a demonstration facility in Albuquerque, New Mexico, designed to hit that mark
by 2030
([Pacific Fusion](https://www.pacificfusion.com/updates/pacific-fusion-breaks-ground-on-first-of-a-kind-fusion-facility-in-new-mexico),
[TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/25/pacific-fusion-next-fusion-machine-could-clear-key-hurdle-to-commercial-power/)).

It's a distinction that matters more than it sounds. Lawrence Livermore's
National Ignition Facility has already shown a fusion reaction can put out
more energy than the lasers pour into the fuel — a genuine milestone, achieved
in 2022. But NIF's own lasers draw far more power from the wall than that
reaction produces, once you count the whole facility. No one has closed that
larger loop yet. Pacific Fusion is trying to be first.

The company's approach is pulser-driven inertial fusion: banks of coordinated
electrical pulses generate a magnetic field around a fuel target roughly the
size of a pencil eraser, compressing it until the atoms inside fuse. The
demonstration system will be built from 156 pulser modules, each with 32
circular stages, engineered to be mass-manufacturable rather than bespoke —
the same modular logic that let solar panels and lithium batteries get cheap.
In June, a one-third-scale prototype of that hardware delivered 440 gigawatts
in an 80-nanosecond burst
([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/06/02/pacific-fusions-latest-prototype-packs-440-gigawatts-into-an-80-nanosecond-burst/)),
evidence the company points to as proof the full-scale design will work.

The New Mexico system is built to fire a handful of shots a day and produce
bursts exceeding 100 megajoules — roughly ten times NIF's output. A
commercial power plant, by contrast, would need five times the demonstration
system's energy per shot and would have to fire about once per second, a gap
Pacific Fusion is aiming to close by the mid-2030s. "This exists nowhere in
the world," said chief technology officer Keith LeChien, who added: "America
pioneered the breakthroughs that brought fusion within reach... America can
still build the hard things."

The project comes with real infrastructure behind it: a $1 billion investment
in the Albuquerque campus, a separate manufacturing site in Los Lunas for the
pulsed-power hardware, and a Memorandum of Understanding with the Department
of Energy's National Nuclear Security Administration for high-yield fusion
research. NNSA Administrator Brandon Williams framed the stakes bluntly:
"China is moving quickly in this area, and America cannot afford to fall
behind." The buildout is expected to create around 200 permanent jobs plus
hundreds more in construction, and the company says it has raised over $1
billion in a milestone-based Series A, with investor Eric Schmidt noting that
"powering the AI era will require bold investment in technologies that can
meet demand at scale."

None of this makes net facility gain a done deal — plenty of fusion ventures
have missed their own timelines before. But breaking ground is a different
kind of commitment than a roadmap slide, and Pacific Fusion COO Carrie von
Muench called it plainly: "a huge step forward for commercial fusion power."
The concrete is going in the ground. Now the company has four years to prove
the physics scales with it.
