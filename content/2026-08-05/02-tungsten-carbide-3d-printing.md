Tungsten carbide-cobalt is the stuff of cutting tools, mining bits,
and aerospace parts — prized for hardness that borders on the
absurd, and priced accordingly, since tungsten is both expensive and
geopolitically concentrated. That combination has made it a poor
candidate for 3D printing: conventional laser additive manufacturing
fully melts metal powder layer by layer, and melting tungsten
carbide tends to introduce cracks and defects that ruin the very
property the material is chosen for.

Researchers at Hiroshima University's Graduate School of Advanced
Science and Engineering, working with Mitsubishi Materials Hardmetal
Corporation, found a way around that trade-off by not fully melting
the material at all. Their hot-wire laser process softens a preheated
filler wire of tungsten carbide-cobalt just enough to fuse it in
place, preserving the microstructure that gives the material its
strength. The result, published August 4 in the *International
Journal of Refractory Metals and Hard Materials*
([Hiroshima University](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/08/260801094053.htm)),
was defect-free samples with hardness above 1400 on the Vickers
scale — squarely in the range needed for industrial cutting tools.

"By using additive manufacturing, cemented carbide can be deposited
only where it is needed, thereby reducing material consumption,"
said Keita Marumoto, the study's corresponding author. That framing
matters as much as the hardness number: because the process builds
up material only where a part actually needs it, rather than
machining a block down from a larger blank, it promises real savings
on a raw material whose cost and supply chain both make waste
expensive in more than one sense.

The technique is still a research result, not a production line —
but for a material this constrained by geography and cost, a printing
method that uses less of it while matching its performance is the
kind of quiet efficiency gain that compounds.
