Lithium manganese-rich (LMR) cathodes have been a tantalizing idea in battery chemistry for years: swap out much of the expensive cobalt and nickel in an EV battery for manganese, one of the cheapest and most abundant metals around, and let the cathode store extra energy by putting oxygen atoms themselves to work in the reaction. The catch has always been that using oxygen this way tends to release gas inside the cell over repeated cycles, swelling and degrading it — a problem serious enough to keep LMR cells out of real vehicles despite two decades of research.

LG Energy Solution and a team led by Professor Jongwoo Lim at Seoul National University's Department of Chemistry say they've found the mechanism behind that gas buildup, and a way to control it. Announced September 7 and detailed in a paper the two groups co-published, the key insight was that gas evolution isn't governed by the upper charging voltage alone, as had been assumed, but by the interaction between the upper charge cutoff and the lower discharge cutoff together
([LG Energy Solution](https://news.lgensol.com/company-news/press-releases/5222/); [The Korea Herald](https://www.koreaherald.com/article/10865485)).
Tuning both in tandem, rather than just capping the top end, suppresses the reaction that releases gas in the first place.

Applied to large-format, 40-amp-hour cells — the scale actually used in EV packs, not just lab coin cells — the technique held onto 92.2% of the battery's initial capacity after 883 full charge-discharge cycles, a level of durability that had eluded LMR chemistry at commercial scale. That's the detail that matters most: plenty of battery chemistries look promising in a small test cell and fall apart when scaled up. Getting this result in a 40 Ah format is what turns a laboratory curiosity into something a cell manufacturer can plausibly build a factory around.

If it holds up in further testing, the payoff is a cathode that sidesteps the cobalt supply chain almost entirely, trims material costs, and still delivers high energy density — the trifecta EV makers have been chasing since lithium iron phosphate started eating into the market for cheaper, if less energy-dense, batteries. Manganese is common enough that switching to it doesn't just lower prices; it loosens one more link in a battery supply chain currently bottlenecked by a handful of countries and materials. Commercialization is still a matter of years, not months, but the specific problem that has stalled LMR chemistry for two decades now has a specific, tested answer.
