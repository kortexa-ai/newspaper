Off the Suffolk coast this week, the last of 95 steel monopiles was driven into
the seabed, closing out the foundation phase of East Anglia THREE, the
biggest offshore windfarm ScottishPower Renewables has ever built. Each
monopile stands 67 to 84 metres tall, weighs 1,200 to 1,800 tonnes, and was
set in place by the Seaway Ventus, the largest jack-up installation vessel
ever to work in European waters. All 95 transition pieces, the collars that
will eventually connect the foundations to the turbine towers, are welded on
too. In total, the project has now put 200,000 tonnes of steel on the floor
of the North Sea
([Renewable Energy Magazine](https://www.renewableenergymagazine.com/wind/major-construction-milestone-completed-at-east-anglia-20260817);
[CleanTechnica](https://cleantechnica.com/2026/08/17/scottishpower-renewables-masdar-mark-major-engineering-milestone-at-east-anglia-three-windfarm/)).

"East Anglia THREE is our biggest windfarm ever, and the completion of the
foundation installation is a true engineering triumph," said Charlie Jordan,
chief executive of ScottishPower Renewables, in announcing the milestone.
Husain Al Meer, Masdar's executive director for global offshore wind and the
UK, called it "a remarkable engineering achievement," and Seaway7 chief
executive Lloyd Duthie credited the joint effort behind it. The three
companies — ScottishPower Renewables and Masdar as the 50:50 developers,
Seaway7 as the installation contractor — have spent nearly two years getting
steel into the water on a project first consented in 2017.

The scale is what makes it matter. Once complete, East Anglia THREE's 95
turbines, carrying some of the longest wind blades ever installed in the UK
at 115 metres each, will generate 1.4 gigawatts, enough to supply more than
1.3 million homes. Turbine installation is already past the halfway mark,
riding on foundations that took years of permitting, seabed surveys, and
supply-chain buildout to reach this point. The developers say the project
has driven almost £2 billion into the UK supply chain and supported 2,300
construction jobs, with roughly 100 permanent roles expected once the
windfarm is running.

None of this is especially glamorous. There's no single dramatic breakthrough
here, no new material or clever algorithm — just the unglamorous, capital-
intensive work of getting very large steel objects into a cold sea, on
schedule, at a scale that used to be rare and is quickly becoming routine.
The UK now has more installed offshore wind capacity than any country except
China, and projects like this one are why: East Anglia THREE is expected to
be fully operational by the end of 2026, delivering carbon-free power to the
grid through a UK winter for the first time. The last monopile going into
the water isn't the finish line, but it's the moment a windfarm stops being
a plan and starts being a machine.
