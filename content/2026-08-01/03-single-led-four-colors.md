Full-color displays have always paid a manufacturing tax: producing
red, green and blue light generally means building and aligning
separate light-emitting layers or separately manufactured chips for
each color, which caps how small and dense a pixel can get.
Researchers at the University of Osaka and Ritsumeikan University have
now shown a way around that, with a single light-emitting layer that
holds four distinct, stable colors — blue, green, yellow and red — at
room temperature ([Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-07-stable-room-temperature.html)).

The team built the LEDs from aluminum gallium nitride doped with
terbium ions, using a manufacturing process already standard for
nitride LEDs, and paired the device with color filters to pull the
separate wavelengths out of the same light-emitting area. The result
held up under a demand that has dogged nitride LEDs for years: color
stability. Conventional green and red nitride LEDs tend to drift in
hue as current changes, but the terbium-doped devices kept their
emission peaks within 0.6 nanometers of target across a range of
drive currents. Adding more aluminum to the crystal also raised the
LEDs' external quantum efficiency — how much of the input energy
actually becomes usable light — by up to a factor of 10.3, work the
team credits to an aluminum nitride base layer that improved crystal
quality and cut down on defects. The results were published in
*Applied Physics Letters*.

"We demonstrated multiple colors from one light-emitting layer,
together with exceptional stability as the current changed," said
senior author Shuhei Ichikawa.

It's a laboratory result, not a product on a shelf, and the road from
a stable four-color LED to a manufacturable display is a long one. But
the appeal is straightforward: fewer components per pixel means denser
pixels are physically possible, and a manufacturing process that
reuses existing nitride LED tooling means the path to scale doesn't
require reinventing the factory. Sometimes progress isn't a new
category of device — it's an old one, quietly asked to do more with
less.
