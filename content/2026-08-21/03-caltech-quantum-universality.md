Water boiling, a magnet losing its magnetism, a superconductor switching
on — on the surface these have nothing in common, but physicists have long
suspected that right at the critical point where each material flips
between phases, they all obey the same underlying mathematics, a property
called universality. A team led by Caltech, working with Université
Paris-Saclay and the Technical University of Munich, has now made the
first direct measurement of the specific energy pattern that theory
predicts should show up at those tipping points, confirming four decades
of prediction with data instead of just equations
([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-08-universal-pattern-revealed-quantum.html)).

The team, led experimentally by Manuel Endres and theoretically by Jason
Alicea, built their test system out of strontium atoms held in place by
laser tweezers and arranged in chains of up to 35. Exciting the atoms into
so-called Rydberg states made them interact strongly with their neighbors,
and a technique the researchers compare to running a wet finger around the
rim of a wine glass to find its resonant tone — formally, many-body
modulation spectroscopy — let them pick out the system's natural energy
frequencies. Those frequencies matched the "energy ladder" that conformal
field theory predicts for the Ising and tricritical Ising universality
classes, once the researchers accounted for the finite size of their atom
chains, and sorting the results by symmetry revealed families of energy
levels that had been hiding in plain sight.

The work, led by graduate researcher Xiangkai Sun and published in Nature
([DOI: 10.1038/s41586-026-10904-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10904-x)),
is less about a single new material than about a new instrument: a
quantum simulator precise enough to test theoretical physics that
ordinary computers cannot calculate directly, because the number of
interacting particles makes exact simulation intractable. The same
approach, the team says, should extend to two-dimensional systems next,
opening a path to studying quantum matter that has so far existed only on
paper.
