In 1936, working out the consequences of the still-young theory of quantum
electrodynamics, Werner Heisenberg and Hans Euler predicted something that
sounded like a contradiction: empty space, under a strong enough magnetic
field, should start behaving like a lens. Light passing through that
vacuum would come out polarized, filtered as if it had passed through a
crystal — even though there was no crystal, no matter, nothing there at
all. The idea followed logically from the physics of the time. Nobody had
the tools to actually see it happen.

Ninety years later, something may have. NASA reported this week that its
Imaging X-ray Polarimetry Explorer, IXPE, spent more than 140 hours
between March and April of 2025 staring at a magnetar called
1E 1547-5408 — a neutron star spinning once every two seconds, wrapped in
a magnetic field roughly a trillion times stronger than the strongest
magnet humans have built
([NASA Science](https://science.nasa.gov/missions/ixpe/nasas-ixpe-may-have-proven-90-year-old-theory/)).
It was the first time anyone had paired X-ray polarization measurements
from IXPE with radio observations from NASA's NICER telescope and CSIRO's
Murriyang radio dish in Australia, watching the same object through two
very different windows at once.

What came back was polarization nearly three times higher than standard
models of a magnetar's surface emission can explain on their own — in
places reaching close to 80 percent, with a subtle dip in the 3 to 4
kiloelectronvolt range that lines up with where quantum electrodynamics
predicts the vacuum's own birefringence should partially cancel out the
polarization coming from the star's surrounding plasma
([arXiv preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19446)). That dip is a
fingerprint, not just a number: it's the specific shape the 1936 theory
said to look for, showing up where the theory said it would.

"The information we obtained from looking at this distant star core also
gives us clues about the nature of the fabric of reality," said Rachael
Stewart, the study's lead author. Co-author Hoa Dinh Thi put it more
technically: matching the data required a model that assumes vacuum
birefringence is actually present in the space around the neutron star.
The results were published in *Nature* on August 5.

NASA and the research team are careful not to oversell it. This is, in
their own description, the most definitive signal yet for vacuum
birefringence — not a closed case. Confirming it will mean pointing IXPE
at 1E 1547-5408 again, and at other magnetars, to see whether the same
fingerprint keeps showing up. But the fact that a shape predicted before
the Second World War, from a theory built on paper and pencil, might now
be visible in starlight collected by a satellite is the kind of thing
that's easy to undersell by calling it "still tentative." Ninety years is
a long time to wait for a look at how the vacuum itself behaves under
pressure most of the universe never sees.
