---
title: A suitcase-sized satellite will hide behind the Moon to hear the universe's dark ages
kicker: Space
author: The Editors
summary: UK researchers unveiled CosmoCube, a small satellite designed to use the Moon itself as a shield from Earth's radio noise while it listens for a faint signal from before the first stars existed.
order: 5
---

Every radio telescope on Earth has the same problem when it tries to listen
for the universe's earliest whispers: our own planet is loud. FM radio,
cell towers, satellite chatter, and the ionosphere itself all drown out the
extremely faint signal astronomers want to hear from the "cosmic dark
ages," the roughly 150-million-year stretch after the Big Bang before the
first stars switched on. A University of Cambridge-led team has a way
around that: put the listening post on the far side of the Moon, with a
whole world of rock in the way
([EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1139660)).

The mission, called CosmoCube, is a satellite roughly the size of a carry-on
suitcase, developed with UK partners Surrey Space Technology, the
University of Portsmouth, and STFC RAL Space, and backed by the UK Space
Agency and the Kavli Foundation. Orbiting beyond the Moon's far side, it
would get about 40 minutes of radio silence per two-hour orbit — long
enough to listen for the 21-centimeter emission line given off by neutral
hydrogen in the young universe, at frequencies between 10 and 50 megahertz
that no ground-based telescope can cleanly access. "The far side of the
Moon is really the only option," said Professor Eloy de Lera Acedo. "It
solves multiple problems at once."

If it flies within the team's five-year target, CosmoCube would spend two
years gathering roughly 1,000 hours of data, aiming to trace how the
universe's first structures, including dark matter's role in gathering the
gas that became the first galaxies, took shape in the dark before there was
any light to see it by.
