Fairphone has spent thirteen years building phones designed to be opened,
fixed, and kept running rather than replaced, and on August 18 that phone
finally went on sale in the United States the ordinary way: through
Fairphone's own store and Amazon, unmodified, for $649
([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/18/fairphone-is-launching-its-latest-repairable-phone-in-the-us-too/);
[9to5Google](https://9to5google.com/2026/08/18/fairphone-gen-6-plus-us-launch-price-specs/)).
Previously, Americans who wanted one had to buy a version altered by a
third-party reseller; the Gen 6+ is the company's first handset it is
selling there directly.

The phone earned a perfect 10 out of 10 on iFixit's repairability scale, a
score almost no mainstream flagship achieves. Twelve components — battery,
display, USB-C port, and camera modules among them — can be swapped out
with nothing more than a single screwdriver, and Fairphone sells the
replacement parts itself. That philosophy hasn't kept the specs behind:
the Gen 6+ ships with a Snapdragon 7s Gen 4 chip, 12GB of DDR5 RAM — a
first for the line — a 6.3-inch OLED display with a variable 120Hz refresh
rate, and 256GB of storage with a microSD slot for more, running on
T-Mobile and AT&T at launch.

The bigger story is what the launch says about the market it's entering.
Repairable, long-lived electronics have mostly been a European
sustainability niche; a right-to-repair-friendly phone company deciding the
US is now worth a direct, unmodified launch — carriers, retail, and all —
is a bet that American buyers are ready to pay a premium for a device they
can actually keep. If Fairphone is right, it won't be the only one making
that bet for long.
