Starship's thirteenth test flight was supposed to be the tidy one. It
turned out to be the interesting one instead — which, for a rocket
program that treats every flight as a data-gathering exercise, is its
own kind of success.

The 397-foot rocket lifted off from SpaceX's Starbase, Texas, at 5:51
p.m. CDT on Friday, July 24, after two false starts: a July 16 attempt
aborted moments after ignition, and a July 23 try scrubbed for weather
([Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/2026/07/)). Once it flew,
Starship met the mission's central goal — deploying 20 next-generation
Starlink V3 satellites, the first time that hardware has flown to space
at all
([Yahoo/Space.com](https://www.yahoo.com/news/science/articles/spacexs-starship-megarocket-makes-softest-014017857.html)).
Because the flight was suborbital by design, the satellites re-entered
shortly after release rather than joining the constellation — this was
a shakeout flight, not an operational one. Six of the twenty carried
cameras and had some heat-shield tiles painted white specifically so
engineers could photograph how the tiles behaved on the way down
([Space.com](https://www.space.com/space-exploration/launches-spacecraft/spacexs-starship-megarocket-makes-the-softest-splashdown-ever-after-launching-next-gen-starlink-satellites-in-flight-13-test-video)).

The booster is where the flight got honest. Super Heavy separated
cleanly and executed its flip maneuver, but during the landing burn
only five of the thirteen engines needed to slow it down actually
relit. It hit the Gulf of Mexico harder than planned, and the camera
feed cut out on impact. And yet SpaceX still logged it as the gentlest
water impact a Super Heavy booster has ever made — a strange kind of
progress, where an engine failure and a record-soft landing happened
in the same ninety seconds
([Yahoo/Space.com](https://www.yahoo.com/news/science/articles/spacexs-starship-megarocket-makes-softest-014017857.html)).

The ship had no such complications. It coasted, deployed its payload,
re-entered, and set down intact in the Indian Ocean in what SpaceX
spokesperson Dan Huot called "the softest splashdown we have ever had
with Starship in the Indian Ocean" — floating upright for a stretch
before tipping over, still transmitting the whole time
([Yahoo/Space.com](https://www.yahoo.com/news/science/articles/spacexs-starship-megarocket-makes-softest-014017857.html)).

None of this makes Starship an operational vehicle yet. SpaceX still
has to prove out full orbital flight, catch a ship with the launch
tower's mechanical arms the way it now routinely catches boosters,
demonstrate in-space propellant transfer, and ready a crewed lunar
lander variant for NASA's Artemis program. But Flight 13 was only the
second outing for the redesigned V3 ship, and it did what early test
flights are for: it flew a real payload, survived a real hardware
problem, and came home in one piece anyway. For a program that has
spent years treating spectacular failures as useful data, a flight
that mixes a booster anomaly with two record-soft splashdowns counts,
by its own logic, as one of the good days.
