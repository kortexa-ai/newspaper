---
title: Physicists steer electrons with light alone, no wires required
kicker: Science
author: The Editors
summary: A University of Michigan team used two colors of laser light to launch and aim electron currents through a semiconductor, with no applied electric field at all.
order: 4
---

Every electronic device relies on some way of pushing electrons in a
chosen direction, and that has almost always meant an applied voltage.
Physicists at the University of Michigan demonstrated a different way:
using nothing but light to launch a current and steer where it goes,
a result they're calling an "electron lighthouse"
([ScienceDaily, July 27](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/07/260727214601.htm);
[University of Michigan News](https://news.umich.edu/electron-lighthouse-illuminates-new-physics/)).

The trick relies on quantum interference. The team fires two colors of
laser light at a semiconductor at once, and each color drives electrons
to the same final energy state by a different absorption pathway. Where
those two pathways overlap, they behave like overlapping ripples on
water: in some directions they reinforce each other, in others they
cancel out, and the result is a beam of electrons that sets off in a
specific direction with no electric field pushing it there at all. Spin
the polarization of the two light beams, and the beam of electrons
sweeps around like an actual lighthouse — hence the name. The work,
published in *Physical Review Letters*, describes a previously
unobserved behavior in semiconductor physics
([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-07-lasers-aim-electron-currents-semiconductor.html)).

It's a fundamental physics result, not a shipping product, and the
researchers are candid that turning it into a device is a separate,
harder problem. But an all-optical way to launch and direct current
without wires or applied voltage points toward new kinds of ultrafast
optical sensors, communications hardware, and imaging systems — places
where light and electronics currently have to be stitched together
clumsily. Sometimes the most useful thing physics can hand engineers
isn't a component, but a genuinely new way to move electrons around.
