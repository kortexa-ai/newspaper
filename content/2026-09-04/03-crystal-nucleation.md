---
title: A century-old theory of how crystals form just got a rewrite
kicker: Science
author: The Editors
summary: By watching individual atoms during a flash-freeze, UCLA researchers found that crystals don't snap into being with a sharp edge — they fade in through a gradient.
order: 3
---

Nearly every solid material you touch got that way by crystallizing from a
disordered state — atoms in a liquid or vapor settling into an ordered
lattice. The textbook account of how that starts, classical nucleation
theory, has stood for close to a century: disordered atoms cross a sharp
boundary into a small, fully ordered nucleus, and once that nucleus is big
enough, the crystal grows from there. A team led by Jianwei "John" Miao at
UCLA has now looked at that boundary atom by atom, in three dimensions, and
found that it isn't sharp at all
([Nature Materials](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41563-026-02727-y), published August 25;
reported by [SciTechDaily](https://scitechdaily.com/scientists-just-challenged-a-century-old-theory-of-how-crystals-form/)
and [UCLA California NanoSystems Institute](https://cnsi.ucla.edu/update-the-textbooks-ucla-led-research-revises-theory-of-how-crystals-form/)).

The technique behind the finding, atomic electron tomography, reconstructs
the three-dimensional positions of individual atoms from electron-microscope
images rather than inferring structure statistically. Miao's team heated
nanoparticles made of complex, high-entropy alloys to roughly 3,000°F and
flash-cooled them back to room temperature in hundredths of a second — fast
enough to catch nucleation in the act — then examined more than 8,000 of the
resulting nuclei individually.

What they saw didn't match the textbook. "The crystals were not uniform
with a sharp boundary from the disordered atoms around them, as predicted
by classical nucleation theory," Miao said. "Instead, we saw a gradient."
Rather than one energy barrier that atoms either cross or don't, the data
pointed to a gradient nucleation pathway: multiple intermediate stages of
partial order, a smoother route from disorder to crystal than the old
sharp-boundary model allowed.

It's a basic-science result, and its authors aren't claiming an application
yet. But nucleation theory underpins how metallurgists design alloys, how
pharmaceutical chemists control which crystal form a drug compound takes,
and how materials scientists predict when a metal will fail. A
hundred-year-old assumption at the bottom of all that just moved.
