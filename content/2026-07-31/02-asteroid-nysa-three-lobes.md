title: An asteroid turned out to have three lobes and a moon nobody knew about
kicker: Science
author: The Editors
summary: Ground-based telescopes in Arizona and Chile combined forces to reveal (44) Nysa as a shape astronomers say they've never documented before — with a kilometer-wide companion hiding in its glare.
order: 2
---

Asteroid (44) Nysa has been known to astronomers since 1857, which makes
it a strange thing to discover something entirely new about now. But a
team led by Kate Minker of Lowell Observatory, combining the Large
Binocular Telescope in Arizona with the European Southern Observatory's
Very Large Telescope in Chile, found that Nysa isn't the simple oblong
rock it was assumed to be. It has three distinct lobes, joined by two
neck-like valleys — a shape the team says has never been documented on
any asteroid before — and, tucked into the glare of the main body, a
moon roughly a kilometer wide that nobody had spotted until now
([Lowell Observatory](https://lowell.edu/press-release-asteroid-44-nysa-may-be-the-first-known-three-lobed-world/);
[Space.com](https://www.space.com/astronomy/asteroids/1st-three-headed-asteroid-found-in-our-solar-system-and-it-has-a-little-moon)).

Finding the moon took borrowing a trick from an entirely different
corner of astronomy. "We borrowed a specialized technique from another
field of astronomy, known as 'high-contrast imaging', to detect the
small moon whose faint light was overwhelmed by the intense brightness
of the primary asteroid," said Gianluca Li Causi of Italy's national
astrophysics institute, whose SHARK-VIS instrument did the work. Minker
called the combination of instruments and processing "the closest
competition to spacecraft-quality imaging ever achieved from the
ground." Nysa itself, at roughly 75 kilometers across, is most likely
either a "contact trinary" of three bodies stuck together or, Minker
said, "an extremely irregular coherent body unlike anything we've
previously observed" — the probable aftermath of a gentle, low-speed
collision billions of years ago rather than a violent one.

The paper, posted this week, is a small reminder that the solar system
still keeps surprises in plain sight: an object cataloged for nearly
170 years, watched by professional telescopes the whole time, just
handed over a shape and a satellite nobody had noticed.
