---
title: Singapore writes the rulebook for cooling data centers in the tropics
kicker: Climate
author: The Editors
summary: A new national standard for liquid-cooled data centers, the first built for hot, humid climates, could cut cooling energy use by more than 30 percent as AI demand drives a construction boom.
order: 4
---

Air cooling was never going to work well in the tropics, and it works even
less well now that AI data centers pack in far more computing power per
rack than anything the grid was designed around. On August 27, Singapore's
Infocomm Media Development Authority and Enterprise Singapore published SS
726:2026, a national standard for liquid cooling in tropical data centers
that both agencies describe as the first of its kind in the world
([Enterprise Singapore](https://www.enterprisesg.gov.sg/resources/media-centre/media-releases/2026/august/mr03826_singapore-launches-worlds-first-liquid-cooling-standard-for-data-centres-in-tropical-climates)).

Liquid cooling moves heat away from chips more efficiently than air, and
can cut a data center's cooling energy use by more than 30 percent
compared with conventional systems
([Tech Wire Asia](https://techwireasia.com/2026/08/singapore-liquid-cooling-standard-ai-data-centres/)).
But high heat and humidity bring their own problems — chiefly corrosion —
that standards written for temperate climates never had to account for.
SS 726:2026 covers the full lifecycle of a liquid-cooled system: planning,
design, deployment, operation, maintenance and risk management, developed
through the Singapore Standards Council in consultation with industry
([Techgoondu](https://www.techgoondu.com/2026/08/28/singapore-launches-worlds-first-tropical-data-centre-liquid-cooling-standard/)).

Singapore has particular reason to get this right. Land- and
power-constrained, it has spent years courting data center operators while
limiting how much electricity they can draw, making every watt saved on
cooling a watt available for compute. A standard that lets liquid-cooled
facilities be built, operated and maintained safely in tropical conditions
gives operators across South and Southeast Asia — where most of the
world's hot, humid, rapidly digitizing cities actually are — a template
they didn't have before.
