---
title: Saturn's other pole has grown a shape of its own
kicker: Space
author: The Editors
summary: Forty years after Voyager found a hexagon at Saturn's north pole, Hubble has confirmed a ten-sided wave at the south pole — and this one is still growing.
order: 2
---

Saturn's north pole has spent four decades as one of the solar system's
best-loved oddities: a hexagon, traced by a jet stream, spotted by the
Voyager probes in the early 1980s and still turning today, long treated as
a geometric fluke unique to that pole. An international team led by
Agustín Sánchez-Lavega of the University of the Basque Country has now
shown it isn't unique at all. Writing in *Science Advances*, the
researchers confirm a ten-sided wave, a decagon, wrapped around Saturn's
south pole
([Science Advances](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aee4251);
reported by [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/09/260903064229.htm)
and [Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/a-mysterious-decagon-unexpectedly-appeared-around-saturns-south-pole-the-ten-sided-shape-seems-to-be-a-wave-in-the-atmosphere-180989449/)).

The decagon was first caught not by a space agency but by amateur
astronomers, Trevor Barry and Jean-Paul Oger, who noticed it in their own
images in 2024. Hubble observations then confirmed it and traced its
presence back to 2023 — young by planetary standards, and unlike the
placid, stable hexagon, still visibly strengthening. "This feature is
different — it appears to be strengthening, giving us the rare opportunity
to watch a giant atmospheric pattern develop," said NASA's Amy Simon, a
team member; the research also drew on collaborators from Goddard Space
Flight Center, UC Berkeley, and several Spanish institutions.

The scientific payoff is bigger than a second pretty shape. A hexagon
explained away as a one-time accident is harder to dismiss once a second
polygon shows up at the opposite pole, on a different timeline, through the
same jet-stream physics. As Sánchez-Lavega put it, the finding suggests the
"unique" hexagon "is not as extraordinary as we thought" — these polygonal
standing waves may simply be what a fast, banded atmosphere like Saturn's
does near its poles, and other gas giants might be shaping quiet geometry
of their own that no one has caught yet.

For now, astronomers get to watch a live experiment: a wave still finding
its shape, on a planet that has already kept one nearly identical secret for
forty years.
