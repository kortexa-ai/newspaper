A Falcon 9 lifted off from Cape Canaveral at 3:49 a.m. EDT on August
5, carrying three AST SpaceMobile BlueBird satellites — numbers 11,
12, and 13 — into low Earth orbit
([Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/2026/08/05/live-coverage-spacex-to-launch-3-ast-spacemobile-bluebird-satellites-on-falcon-9-rocket-from-cape-canaveral/)).
The booster, flying its 30th mission, landed on the droneship *A
Shortfall of Gravitas* soon after — the 645th successful Falcon
booster landing to date, a routine that has become so reliable it
barely makes news anymore, which is itself a kind of achievement.

The payload is the more interesting part. AST SpaceMobile's BlueBird
satellites are built to connect ordinary, unmodified smartphones
directly to a phone network from orbit — no special antenna or
terminal required, the way satellite phones have always demanded.
Each satellite unfolds to roughly 2,400 square feet, carrying what
the company describes as the largest commercial phased array flown in
low Earth orbit, aimed at closing cellular dead zones that terrestrial
towers don't reach: rural roads, disaster areas, stretches of ocean.

CEO Abel Avellan has described the program's core bet as
manufacturing at scale — a lightweight, stackable satellite design
built to be produced and launched repeatedly rather than as one-off
hardware. That bet is visibly paying off in cadence: with BlueBirds
11 through 13 now in orbit, the company says production is already
running through unit 42, with the next three satellites due to
launch imminently. A constellation that reaches ordinary phones
without asking anyone to buy new hardware is a quiet but real step
toward treating connectivity as something that shouldn't depend on
how close you live to a cell tower.
