Voyager 2 has been flying since 1977, and for most of the last decade
its story has been one of subtraction. The spacecraft runs on
radioisotope thermoelectric generators — devices that turn the heat
of decaying plutonium into electricity — and that plutonium is always,
quietly, running out. Each year the probe loses about four watts of
power it will never get back. Since 2024, the mission has already
had to switch off two of its science instruments on each of the twin
Voyager spacecraft just to keep the lights on for the rest.

This week, NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory announced it found a way
to buy back some of that lost time instead of just spending down what
remains
([NASA Science](https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/voyager/2026/08/04/nasa-engineers-help-prolong-voyager-2s-science-mission/);
[JPL](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasa-engineers-help-prolong-voyager-2s-science-mission/)).
Engineers nicknamed the effort the "Big Bang": rather than shutting
down instruments one at a time as power dwindled, the team
simultaneously turned off select powered devices and swapped others
for lower-power alternatives, all while keeping the spacecraft's
remaining hardware warm enough to keep functioning in the cold of
interstellar space. It's a coordinated, one-time reshuffling of a
fixed and shrinking power budget, done carefully enough that nothing
essential goes dark in the process.

The payoff is concrete: without the Big Bang, the mission would have
had to power down another one of Voyager 2's three remaining science
instruments before the end of 2026. Instead, the savings should keep
all three running for at least another year. That's not a dramatic
number by the standards of a news cycle built around funding rounds
and rocket launches, but for a 49-year-old spacecraft billions of
miles from home, a year is not a small thing — it's a year of solar
wind and cosmic ray data from a region of space that only two human-
made objects have ever reached, and that nothing else is currently on
its way to reach again.

The team isn't finished. The plan is to perform the same power swap
on Voyager 1 in the coming months — trickier, JPL notes, because
Voyager 1 is farther from Earth, which means every command and every
confirmation takes longer to make the round trip. Both spacecraft
are managed by a small team of engineers who, by now, are essentially
practicing a specialized form of triage: deciding, watt by watt, what
stays on and what doesn't, for hardware that can't be repaired,
replaced, or even inspected up close ever again.

There's no version of this story where the Voyagers run forever. The
plutonium keeps decaying regardless of how cleverly the power is
managed, and at some point — not this year, but not infinitely far
off either — the instruments will go quiet for good. What the Big
Bang maneuver represents is something narrower and, in its way, more
impressive: a team of engineers looking at a problem with no
new-hardware solution and finding real, additional runway anyway,
using nothing but the ingenuity of how the existing hardware is run.
Voyager 2 will keep sending data home for a while longer because
people on Earth got clever about arithmetic.
