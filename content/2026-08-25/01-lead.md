On Friday, mission managers from NASA and SpaceX gathered at Kennedy Space
Center and signed off on the last formal checkpoint before launch. The
Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, examined instrument by instrument
alongside its rocket and launch site, is cleared to fly no earlier than
7:26 a.m. EDT on Sunday, August 30, aboard a Falcon Heavy from Launch
Complex 39A
([NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/roman/2026/08/21/teams-complete-flight-readiness-review-for-nasas-roman-telescope/),
[SpaceNews](https://spacenews.com/roman-space-telescope-on-track-for-late-august-launch/)).
Teams completed a full dress rehearsal the day before, running the
countdown from propellant load to simulated liftoff.

Roman is named for the astronomer who ran NASA's first satellite
astronomy programs and pushed hardest for what became the Hubble Space
Telescope — colleagues called her the mother of Hubble long before this
observatory carried her name. Where Hubble and the James Webb Space
Telescope study small patches of sky in exquisite detail, Roman is built
for breadth: a field of view roughly a hundred times wider than Hubble's,
at comparable sharpness, so it can survey enormous swaths of the infrared
sky in a single exposure rather than one galaxy at a time.

That breadth is the point. Two of Roman's core surveys will watch for the
faint gravitational bending of starlight to find exoplanets that other
methods miss, including free-floating worlds with no star at all, and
will map the distribution of galaxies across billions of years to
measure how dark energy has shaped the universe's expansion. A
coronagraph instrument riding along will directly image gas-giant
planets around nearby stars, a hard technical feat that has only
recently become possible.

None of that starts right away. After liftoff, Roman faces a three-month
cruise to the Sun-Earth L2 point, the same gravitationally stable
vantage point Webb occupies, followed by roughly three more months
cooling down and calibrating its instruments. Only then does its primary
mission begin: five years of surveying, expected to produce a data set
so large that NASA has built much of its analysis pipeline around
letting other astronomers mine it long after the observing is done
([NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/roman-space-telescope/roman-launch-countdown/)).

The mission has been a long time coming — first recommended by
astronomers in 2010, formally started in 2016, and repeatedly a target
for cancellation in federal budget proposals it survived each time. What
changes now is that the argument is over. The hardware is built, tested,
and, as of this week, cleared to fly. If the countdown holds, one of the
most ambitious infrared observatories ever built leaves Florida in five
days, carrying instruments designed to answer questions about dark
energy and distant worlds that Hubble and Webb were never built to
tackle
([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/08/260823094140.htm)).
