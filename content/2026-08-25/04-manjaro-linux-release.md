Manjaro 26.1.1 shipped on August 24, the first point release in the
distribution's 26.1 "Bian-May" series. The headline change is Linux
kernel 7.2 joining Manjaro's list of officially supported kernels,
alongside 7.1.9 and the usual run of long-term-support branches — 6.18,
6.12, 6.6, and 6.1 — for users who'd rather not move
([Linuxiac](https://linuxiac.com/manjaro-26-1-1-lands-with-linux-kernel-7-2-kde-frameworks-6-29/)).

The rest of the update is the routine churn of a rolling-adjacent distro
staying current: KDE Frameworks 6.29, Mesa 26.1.7 for graphics drivers,
QEMU 11.1 for virtualization, and refreshed builds of Firefox, Thunderbird,
and VirtualBox. None of it is a headline on its own. Together, it's the
kind of release that doesn't make news because it's supposed to be
boring — a distribution doing the unglamorous work of tracking upstream
so the millions of small choices a kernel and a desktop environment make
under the hood don't quietly go stale under its users.

Manjaro has spent years positioning itself as Arch Linux with the sharp
edges filed down: the same rolling access to current software, without
requiring users to hand-assemble their own system first. Point releases
like this one are where that promise gets tested in practice, not in
marketing — whether the newest kernel and the newest desktop actually
land together, cleanly, for people who never read a changelog and just
expect their laptop to keep working.
