---
title: Solar and batteries are now most of what America is building
kicker: Climate
author: The Editors
summary: Solar and storage combined for 91% of all new US grid capacity in early 2026 — the highest share either technology has ever posted, alone or together.
order: 3
---

The story of the American power grid this year is mostly a story about
what utilities are choosing to build. In the first quarter of 2026, solar
and battery storage together accounted for 91% of all new electricity
generating capacity added nationwide — the largest combined share on
record for the two technologies
([SEIA, via ess-news](https://www.ess-news.com/2026/07/23/solar-and-storage-drive-us-grid-growth-in-h1-with-record-q1-capacity-share/),
corroborated by [CleanTechnica](https://cleantechnica.com/2026/07/16/6-records-solar-storage-have-crushed-in-the-first-half-of-2026/)
and [pv magazine USA](https://pv-magazine-usa.com/2026/07/22/solar-and-storage-account-for-91-of-new-u-s-grid-capacity-in-first-half-of-2026/)).

The milestones stack up. The US likely crossed 300 gigawatts of
cumulative installed solar capacity for the first time during the first
half of the year, with solar now supplying roughly 13% of the country's
electricity. In May, monthly solar generation exceeded coal generation
nationwide for the first time in US history. Regional grid operators are
setting their own records on top of the national trend: California's
CAISO saw its battery fleet discharge a record 12.99 gigawatts on July 9,
covering more than a third of demand after sunset, then broke its solar
record the next day at 23 gigawatts, meeting 72% of afternoon demand.
Texas's ERCOT logged its own solar peak of over 35 gigawatts the same
week.

None of this is a one-quarter fluke. Federal Energy Regulatory Commission
projections cited in the reporting point to more than 86 gigawatts of new
utility-scale solar and nearly 20 gigawatts of wind coming online between
now and 2028. Batteries, which spent years as solar's supporting act,
have become central to the pitch on their own — letting a grid built
around an intermittent source deliver power reliably after dark. The
technologies that were once the alternative to the grid are, increasingly,
just the grid.
