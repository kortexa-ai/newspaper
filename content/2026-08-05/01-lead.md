The ocean already does a version of carbon removal for free: it
absorbs roughly a quarter of the carbon dioxide humans emit each year,
dissolving it into seawater. The trouble has always been what to do
with it once it's there. Pull the CO2 back out electrochemically and
the reaction tends to coat the electrodes in mineral scale within
hours, killing the very process meant to run for months. A team at
KAIST, working with T. Alan Hatton's group at MIT, says it has found a
way around that failure mode — and published the result on August 4
([EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1138652);
[KAIST News Center](https://news.kaist.ac.kr/newsen/html/news/?mng_no=42710))
in *Advanced Energy Materials*
([Wiley](https://advanced.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/aenm.71205)).

The technology, called electrochemical dissolved ocean carbon removal,
or e-DOC, converts the carbon dioxide dissolved in seawater into
calcium carbonate — ordinary limestone, chemically speaking — a
mineral stable enough that it simply will not return to the
atmosphere. That's a meaningfully different proposition from capturing
gaseous CO2 and compressing it into underground storage: the carbon
here ends up locked into rock at the outset, no pipeline or
sequestration site required.

The team's fix for the scaling problem is mechanical as much as
chemical. Led by professor Dong-Yeun Koh, the researchers built what
they call a hollow fiber electrode assembly: bundles of thin, hollow,
thread-like electrodes arranged so that mineral crystals form just
outside the electrode surface rather than directly on it. Hydrogen
bubbles generated by the same reaction do the rest, acting as a
constant brush that keeps the electrode clear as new mineral forms
around — not on — it. In testing, the system ran continuously for
more than 120 hours, a span past which earlier electrochemical designs
have typically clogged and stopped.

The efficiency numbers are what make the result more than a lab
curiosity. The system removed 80 to 90 percent of the dissolved
inorganic carbon in the seawater it processed, while cutting
electricity consumption by up to 54 percent compared with existing
electrochemical ocean-capture approaches — the single biggest cost
lever for a technology that ultimately has to compete on price per
ton of carbon removed. It also throws off two useful byproducts along
the way: high-purity hydrogen and magnesium hydroxide, both of which
have industrial value of their own and could help offset the cost of
running the system.

Koh's team designed the hardware to be compact enough for deployment
on ships and offshore platforms, which matters because the ocean's
carbon-holding capacity is enormous and largely untapped by land-based
capture infrastructure. A system that can sit on a vessel and process
seawater directly, rather than requiring CO2 to be piped ashore first,
opens up carbon removal at a scale that's hard to reach any other way.

None of this makes ocean carbon removal a finished technology —
120 hours is a strong proof of continuous operation, not a
demonstration of years-long durability at industrial volumes, and the
path from a lab-scale hollow fiber assembly to a fleet of shipboard
units remains to be built. But the specific problem that has kept
electrochemical ocean capture stuck at the demonstration stage —
electrodes fouling within hours — appears to have a real answer now,
and the energy savings suggest the economics are moving in the right
direction too.
