---
title: A chip the size of a grain of rice made a rainbow that could carry 6G
kicker: Science
author: The Editors
summary: Physicists at Loughborough University built a microcomb that generates precisely spaced light frequencies, a building block for future 6G links and quantum-precise timing.
order: 3
---

A microcomb is exactly what it sounds like: a beam of light split into
many frequencies, evenly spaced like the teeth of a comb, all locked
together with extreme precision. Physicists at Loughborough University's
Emergent Photonics Research Centre, working with researchers at the
University of Sussex, City University of Hong Kong, and QXP Technologies,
have built one on a chip small enough to sit on a grain of rice — and
shown it can be converted into multiple high-frequency electromagnetic
signals at once, the millimeter waves that next-generation 6G networks
are expected to run on
([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/08/260823014946.htm),
[phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-08-rainbow-chip-6g-networks-precision.html)).

Current wireless networks mostly use a single carrier frequency at a
time. The Loughborough team's device instead produces a broad, tightly
spaced spectrum of light frequencies in one go, which can then be tapped
to generate several millimeter-wave signals simultaneously — more
channels, more capacity, from one tiny source instead of a bank of
separate transmitters. The same precision that makes the comb useful for
communications also makes it useful for timing: because the frequency
spacing is so exact, it can serve as a reference clock accurate enough
for quantum technologies, satellite navigation, and radar, applications
that depend on splitting time far more finely than ordinary electronics
allow.

None of this is a 6G rollout — the work, published this week in Nature
Communications, is a laboratory demonstration of a component, not a
network. But it's the kind of component the field has been short of: a
compact, chip-scale source of the kind of signal that six-generation
wireless is supposed to need. The comb itself has an unglamorous origin
story — the Loughborough team first stumbled onto it in 2025 while
building high-power chips meant for brighter laser beams, then spent the
past year turning the accident into something they could actually
characterize and use
([ScienceDaily, October 2025](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2025/10/251007081823.htm)).
The gap between a promising chip in a lab and a signal tower is still
wide, but it is now a gap with a working part in it.
