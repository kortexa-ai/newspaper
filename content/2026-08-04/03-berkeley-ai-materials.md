Making a new solid material — a battery electrode, a sensor compound,
a ceramic — has long been a matter of trial and error: mix ingredients,
bake them, see what forms, adjust, repeat, sometimes for years. That
slowness has made materials synthesis one of the real bottlenecks
between a promising material discovered on a computer and one anyone
can actually manufacture. Researchers at Lawrence Berkeley National
Laboratory, led by senior scientist Kristin Persson with first author
Vir Karan, have now built a machine learning model that simulates
entire reaction pathways — intermediate compounds, final products, and
unwanted impurities included — in minutes
([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-08-ai-atom-movement-solid-state.html)).

What sets the model apart is that it doesn't just weigh which end
products are thermodynamically favorable, the way earlier approaches
did. It also accounts for kinetics — how atoms actually diffuse
through the disordered, jumbled interfaces where a solid-state reaction
happens — which is often what determines whether a reaction takes the
clean path chemists want or wanders off into a stubborn impurity
instead. The team validated the model against decades of published
experimental data on barium-titanium oxide chemistry, a well-studied
system precisely because it's so often used to check new methods
against reality, and found it matched. The work is published in
*Nature Materials* (DOI:
[10.1038/s41563-026-02596-5](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41563-026-02596-5)).

It's a tool for chemists, not a finished material — but a model that
can rule out years of failed recipes before anyone lights a furnace is
exactly the kind of unglamorous acceleration that lets other
discoveries, in batteries and sensors alike, move faster.
