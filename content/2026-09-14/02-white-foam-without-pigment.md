title: Researchers made things brilliantly white without using any white pigment at all

summary: A Kyoto University team engineered foam structures that scatter light like snow and shed water like a lotus leaf, sidestepping both titanium dioxide and PFAS.
order: 2
---

White is one of those colors that isn't really a color — it's a trick of structure. Snow is colorless ice; it looks white because its countless tiny surfaces scatter every wavelength of light back at once. Industry, though, mostly fakes that trick with titanium dioxide pigment, and waterproofs things with PFAS, the "forever chemicals" that resist breaking down in the environment. A team led by Professor Easan Sivaniah at Kyoto University's Institute for Integrated Cell-Material Sciences, working with collaborators at Tokyo Metropolitan University and Donghua University, just found a way to get both effects — whiteness and water repellency — from structure alone, no pigment and no fluorine chemistry required.

Their method, published in *Nature*, works by exposing a polymer to light, which breaks it into smaller fragments; a mild solvent treatment then lets those fragments reorganize into an open, porous foam. The pores scatter light internally, producing structural whiteness the same way snow or a cotton ball gets its brightness. The same rough, textured surface that creates the pores also repels water, echoing the microscopic bumps that keep a lotus leaf dry ([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-09-white-nature-sustainable-repellent-materials.html); [Nature Index / ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/09/260909231712.htm)).

The timing matters: the European Union recently banned titanium dioxide as a food additive over safety concerns, and PFAS regulation is tightening worldwide as its persistence in soil and water draws scrutiny. "A key challenge faced by biomimetic science is realizing environmentally friendly material designs inspired by nature at the scale and cost of existing materials," said co-author Taiki Yanagishima. The technique, which the team calls Deep Foam Photolithography, can pattern polymer films at up to 20,000 dots per inch, opening the door to pigment- and PFAS-free packaging, textiles and printing — brightness borrowed from physics instead of chemistry.
