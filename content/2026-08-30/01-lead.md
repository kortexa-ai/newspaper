At 7:26 a.m. EDT on Sunday, a SpaceX Falcon Heavy carrying NASA's Nancy
Grace Roman Space Telescope lifted off from Launch Complex 39A at Kennedy
Space Center, its 27 Merlin engines producing more than five million pounds
of thrust
([NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/roman/2026/08/30/nasas-roman-space-telescope-launches/)).
Half an hour later, NASA confirmed the telescope had separated cleanly from
the rocket's second stage — the moment that turns a piece of cargo into a
functioning spacecraft
([NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/roman/2026/08/30/nasa-concludes-roman-space-telescope-launch-coverage/)).

The destination is a point in space rather than a place: the second
Sun-Earth Lagrange point, or L2, about one million miles from Earth, where
the gravity of the Sun and Earth balance in a way that lets a spacecraft
hold a stable position with little fuel
([NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/roman/2026/08/30/nasas-roman-space-telescope-launch-updates/)).
It's the same neighborhood James Webb has called home since 2022, and for
similar reasons — a cold, stable perch far from the light and heat of
Earth is exactly what a sensitive infrared observatory needs.

Roman's design trades Hubble's narrow, deep stare for breadth. It carries a
mirror the same size as Hubble's 2.4 meters, but paired with a camera built
to see a field of view roughly a hundred times wider, so it can survey huge
swaths of sky in a single pointing instead of the patient inch-by-inch work
its predecessors do. Over a five-year primary mission, NASA expects the
telescope to map billions of galaxies, trace the gravitational fingerprints
of dark matter, probe the dark energy accelerating the universe's
expansion, and find roughly 100,000 new exoplanets — with the data made
freely available to scientists and the public
([NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/roman/2026/08/30/nasas-roman-space-telescope-launch-updates/)).

The telescope is named for Nancy Grace Roman, NASA's first chief
astronomer, who helped shepherd the Hubble Space Telescope itself from
proposal to launch decades ago — a fitting namesake for a mission built, in
part, from a donated Hubble-class mirror originally fabricated for a
different purpose entirely.

Sunday's flight was the sixth NASA science mission to fly on a Falcon
rocket out of Kennedy Space Center, part of a track record that has made
SpaceX's heaviest operational launcher a routine ride for the agency's
biggest payloads
([NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/roman/2026/08/30/nasas-roman-space-telescope-launches/)).
Getting here took a formal Launch Readiness Review on Friday, where NASA
and SpaceX managers signed off that the rocket, the observatory and the
weather were all ready to go — the last checkpoint before a countdown
clock starts for real.

None of that guarantees a clean ride the rest of the way. Roman still has
weeks of commissioning ahead — deploying hardware, calibrating instruments,
and firing thrusters to refine its course to L2 — before it can begin the
survey it was built for. But with a good launch and a clean separation
behind it, the telescope is now doing the one thing that matters most for
a spacecraft on its first day: flying, on its own, toward the work.
