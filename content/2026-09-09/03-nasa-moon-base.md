Getting people to the Moon again is the easy part, relatively speaking. Keeping them there is what NASA's newest solicitation, announced September 8, is actually aimed at. Called the NextSTEP-3 Lunar Enabling Infrastructure Accelerator, it calls for industry, university, and nonprofit proposals in five specific areas where the agency says real gaps remain before a sustained human presence at the lunar South Pole is possible
([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-calls-for-proposals-to-accelerate-lunar-surface-technologies/)).

The list reads like a checklist for actually living somewhere rather than just visiting: vertical solar arrays that can generate, manage, and store power through the Moon's brutal two-week nights; systems to pull usable oxygen out of lunar regolith rather than shipping it from Earth; radioisotope Stirling generators for power in permanently shadowed craters where solar panels are useless; in-space manufacturing to cut dependence on resupply missions; and better nanomaterials production for construction and equipment on-site. None of these are the kind of thing that makes for a dramatic launch broadcast, and that's rather the point — they're the plumbing a base needs before anyone can stay past the length of a single mission.

"NASA is accelerating the development of key technologies and closing critical gaps needed for long-term human exploration at the Moon," said Greg Stover, director of NASA's Advanced Research and Technology Division. "Partnering with industry will strengthen the U.S. industrial base as we mature the capabilities and infrastructure needed for a sustainable lunar presence."

It's a solicitation, not a launch — proposals, not hardware, are what's due next. But it's also an honest admission of what's actually been missing from lunar planning: not rockets, which multiple companies can now build, but the boring infrastructure of staying somewhere with no atmosphere, no water, and two-week nights. Opening that work up to industry and academia, rather than trying to solve it all in-house, is how that kind of unglamorous progress tends to get made.
