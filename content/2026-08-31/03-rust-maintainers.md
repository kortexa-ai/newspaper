title: Rust starts paying the people who quietly keep it running
kicker: Open Source
author: The Editors
summary: The Rust Foundation is funding six maintainers of cargo, rustup, Clippy and the compiler itself, turning volunteer labor that underpins the language into paid, sustained work.
order: 3
---

Most of the infrastructure a programming language depends on is invisible
right up until it breaks: the package manager, the linter, the installer,
the parts of the compiler nobody thinks about until they stop working. For
years, much of that work in Rust has been done by volunteers fitting it in
around day jobs. On August 26, the Rust Foundation announced its first
Maintainers in Residence — six contributors who will now be paid to do
that work directly
([Rust Blog](https://blog.rust-lang.org/2026/08/26/announcing-our-first-maintainers-in-residence/)).

Gen Li, who has led the `rustup` project since 2025, becomes the program's
first full-time hire. Chris Denton, Alejandra González and León Liehr take
half-time roles covering the standard library and Windows support,
Clippy's performance and review backlog, and rustdoc alongside the
compiler's type system, respectively. Jonas Böttiger and Jason Newcomb
join as maintainer grantees, each funded for roughly a day a week on the
standard library and Clippy bug fixes. The funding, drawn from the Rust
Foundation Maintainers Fund with contributions from Google, AWS, OpenAI
and the Rust Project Leadership Council, runs for twelve months
([Rust Blog](https://blog.rust-lang.org/2026/08/26/announcing-our-first-maintainers-in-residence/)).

"Funding is the system that helps me pour my heart into a project without
worrying about making ends meet," González said. Li put it more simply:
"Turning volunteering into an actual job has really been an empowering
experience so far!"

It's a small program by dollar terms, but it targets exactly the teams —
cargo, rustup, the compiler, Clippy, rustfmt, rust-analyzer — that
millions of Rust developers touch every day without ever knowing their
names. Paying people to maintain the tools everyone else builds on is one
of open source's oldest unsolved problems. This is one language, betting
that a little money aimed precisely can go a long way toward solving it.
