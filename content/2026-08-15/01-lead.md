For decades, hitting a trillion neutrons in a single fusion shot was a
number only national laboratories could claim. Building the capacitor
banks, switches, and diagnostics needed to compress and pinch a plasma
hard enough to fuse deuterium nuclei at that scale required budgets that
only governments tended to have. Earlier this month, a private company
said it got there too — and did it more efficiently than the government
benchmark it was chasing.

Fuse Energy Technologies reported that its dense plasma focus device,
FAETON-X, produced a peak yield of 1.27 trillion neutrons in a single
deuterium-deuterium shot, drawing on roughly one megajoule of stored
energy discharged through a capacitor bank at 65 kilovolts and more than
4.5 megaamps of peak current. The company says it's the first
publicly documented result at that scale by a commercial fusion outfit,
and that the yield per megajoule beats Lawrence Livermore National
Laboratory's MJOLNIR facility — long the reference point for dense
plasma focus performance — by about 40 percent
([Research Square preprint](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-10657590/v1);
[PowerMag](https://www.powermag.com/fuse-touts-highest-neutron-yield-by-any-fusion-company/);
[The Fusion Report](https://thefusionreport.substack.com/p/this-weeks-fusion-news-august-14)).

Worth saying plainly: this is a company-reported result posted as a
preprint, not yet through peer review, and there's no independent
replication yet. It should be read as a strong claim from a credible
lab, not a settled fact — the kind of number that earns scrutiny rather
than a headline about fusion being solved. Dense plasma focus devices
work differently from the tokamaks and laser-inertial systems that
usually make fusion news: they pinch a plasma column with a pulsed
magnetic field rather than confining it magnetically for long stretches
or imploding a fuel pellet with lasers. The approach is mechanically
simpler and cheaper to iterate on, which is part of why a private
company with a modest budget could plausibly compete with a national
lab's flagship machine at all.

FAETON-X is the scaled-up successor to Fuse's pilot device, FAETON-I,
which ran at a tenth of the energy. The company says it went from
design to first shot on the new machine in nine months, then spent
another nine months tuning it before this shot. Fuse isn't waiting on
fusion power to have a business model — it already sells radiation
effects testing to defense and aerospace customers who need
high-neutron-flux environments to qualify hardware, which gives it
revenue while it works toward the harder goal of net energy gain.
"Fusion has reached an inflection point, and private companies are now
at the forefront," CEO JC Btaiche said of the result.

That inflection point is still a long way from a power plant. A
trillion-plus neutrons in a single pulse says something about how
efficiently a machine can compress and heat a plasma, not that it's
producing more energy than it consumes — dense plasma focus devices
have historically struggled to close that gap. But efficiency gains
compound, and a small company beating a national lab's benchmark on its
own terms, with a fraction of the budget, is the kind of result that
tends to pull more attention, more capital, and more competition into a
field that badly needs all three.
