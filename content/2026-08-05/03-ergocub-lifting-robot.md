Most robots are built the way most software used to be: design the
hardware first, then write the control code to make it work. A team
from the Italian Institute of Technology, GenerativeBionics, and the
University of Manchester tried the opposite approach — designing a
humanoid robot's physical structure and its control system together,
as one problem — and published the results August 5 in *Nature
Machine Intelligence*
([techxplore](https://techxplore.com/news/2026-08-human-aware-robots-partners-strain.html)),
DOI 10.1038/s42256-026-01272-2.

"Nature offers a very different perspective," said co-author Daniele
Pucci. "In humans and animals, the body and intelligence evolve
together in response to the environment." The team, led by Pucci and
Carlotta Sartore, applied that logic to a concrete, unglamorous task:
two people (or a person and a robot) lifting something together,
which is one of the more common ways backs get injured in warehouses
and factories.

The resulting robot, ergoCub, was tested in physical collaboration
with human partners on real lifting tasks. Jointly optimizing its
build and its control logic measurably reduced the estimated torque
on the human partner's lumbosacral joint — the lower-back hinge point
most exposed during lifting — compared with two people lifting alone.
The same redesigned body also moved faster and more efficiently than
its predecessor, a reminder that a form built for its intended job
tends to outperform one adapted to it after the fact.

It's a research result, not a warehouse-ready product, and there's a
long road between one experimental robot and a fleet of them on a
factory floor. But the underlying idea — that a robot's body should
be shaped by the people it works alongside, not just the tasks it
performs — points toward assistive machines that fit human work
rather than merely automating around it.
