A Falcon 9 lifted off from Cape Canaveral before dawn on August 5,
carrying three more BlueBird satellites for AST SpaceMobile — numbers 11,
12, and 13 — into low Earth orbit, with more already in production
through satellite 42 ([GCN](https://gcn.com/ast-spacemobile-puts-bluebird-satellites-orbit/20493/)).

The pitch behind the constellation is straightforward: ordinary
smartphones, no special hardware, connected directly to a satellite when
there's no terrestrial tower in range. The newest BlueBirds carry phased
array antennas covering nearly 2,400 square feet, up from 693 square feet
on the earlier generation, which AST SpaceMobile says makes them the
largest commercial phased arrays flying in low Earth orbit. The company
is building toward beta service with AT&T and Verizon later this year;
continuous coverage across key markets will need somewhere between 45 and
60 satellites in orbit, a threshold AST SpaceMobile expects to reach by
early 2027.

Thirteen satellites in, the network still has gaps — coverage today is
intermittent, not continuous, and each launch is one more piece of a
constellation that isn't finished yet. But the trajectory is the story:
a technology that started as a promise to reach hikers, rural residents,
and disaster zones with no signal is now visibly, launch by launch,
closing the distance between a phone with bars and a phone with none.
