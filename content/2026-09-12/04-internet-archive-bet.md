BET recently removed its entire back catalog of digital news articles from its website, choosing to focus on video content and leaving years of written journalism about Black culture, music, and history with no live home on the internet. It's the kind of quiet deletion that normally just happens — a company's business decision, a writer's byline gone, no announcement, no recourse.

Except this time, it wasn't actually gone. The Internet Archive announced this week that it has preserved more than 199,000 BET articles collected over the years through its Wayback Machine crawls, and it built a new pilot tool, the BET Explorer, to make that specific archive easy to search and browse rather than leaving it buried in the general Wayback index ([Internet Archive](https://x.com/internetarchive/status/2097400263656996883); [Library Journal infoDOCKET](https://www.infodocket.com/2026/09/08/internet-archive-releases-archive-of-bet-journalism-removed-from-the-web/); [BET Explorer](https://wayback-labs.sf.archive.org/bet-explorer/)).

It's a small, specific rescue, and it's also a preview of an increasingly common one. As more publishers restructure around video or shut down outright, the written record of entire beats — culture writing, local news, specialist journalism — is only one server migration away from disappearing outright. The Internet Archive's day job is exactly this unglamorous kind of custodianship: crawling the web before it changes its mind, then making sure the copy is actually usable when someone needs it. This week, someone did.
