title: Electrodes shaped like nature's math get a lot better at moving liquid
kicker: Climate
author: The Editors
summary: A University of Waterloo team 3D-printed battery electrodes using minimal-surface geometries found in nature and got a 52 percent performance boost — good news for the batteries best suited to storing wind and solar power at grid scale.
order: 4
---

Redox flow batteries are an unglamorous but promising answer to a hard
problem: storing enormous amounts of wind and solar power cheaply, since
their capacity scales simply by using bigger tanks of liquid electrolyte.
Their weak point has been the electrodes, where that liquid has to reach
reaction surfaces efficiently or the whole system loses performance. A
team at the University of Waterloo, led by chemical engineering professor
Dr. Maxime van der Heijden, used 3D printing to redesign that geometry
from the ground up
([University of Waterloo](https://uwaterloo.ca/news/media/nature-inspired-3d-printing-could-improve-large-scale)).

Using a digital light-processing printer, the researchers built porous
electrodes out of triply periodic minimal surfaces — complex, repeating
three-dimensional shapes that show up throughout the natural world — then
heat-treated them into conductive carbon. After testing several
geometries, a "diamond" pattern came out on top, delivering a 52 percent
performance increase over conventional designs by giving the electrolyte
more efficient pathways to flow through. "With 3D printing, we can design
the internal structure of an electrode in ways that are difficult to
achieve using conventional manufacturing," van der Heijden said. The
research, published in the Journal of Energy Storage, is a reminder that
some of the most useful advances in the energy transition aren't new
chemistries at all, just better-shaped plumbing for the ones we already
have.
