At 7:26 a.m. Eastern on Sunday, a SpaceX Falcon Heavy rocket lifted off from
Launch Complex 39A at Kennedy Space Center carrying NASA's Nancy Grace Roman
Space Telescope, the agency's next flagship observatory and its first
built explicitly to hunt for the fingerprints of dark energy and dark
matter across the cosmos
([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasas-dark-universe-seeking-nancy-grace-roman-space-telescope-launches/)).
The telescope is now three months into the roughly one-million-mile journey
that will carry it to the second Sun-Earth Lagrange point, L2, where it
will settle into an orbit around the Sun rather than the Earth — the same
gravitationally stable neighborhood already occupied by the James Webb
Space Telescope.

Roman shares Hubble's 2.4-meter primary mirror, but its Wide Field
Instrument pairs that mirror with a 300-megapixel infrared camera built
from 18 individual detectors, giving it a field of view roughly 100 times
larger than Hubble's. NASA says that combination lets Roman survey the sky
about 1,000 times faster than its predecessor, and the observatory will
downlink up to 1.4 terabytes of data a day — the highest data rate of any
NASA astrophysics mission to date
([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasas-dark-universe-seeking-nancy-grace-roman-space-telescope-launches/)).
Where Hubble and Webb tend to stare deeply at small patches of sky, Roman
is built to sweep across enormous ones, cataloguing billions of galaxies in
wide, repeated surveys.

That scale is the point. By tracking how galaxies cluster and how their
light bends on its way to us across cosmic time, astronomers hope Roman's
surveys will pin down how dark energy — the mysterious pressure
accelerating the universe's expansion — has behaved over billions of
years, and map the unseen scaffolding of dark matter that holds galaxies
together. The same wide-field surveys are expected to turn up thousands of
new exoplanets, including many found by gravitational microlensing, a
technique that can detect worlds Hubble and Webb are poorly suited to see.

"Delivered ahead of schedule and on budget, this mission reflects more
than a decade of dedication," NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman said of
the launch
([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasas-dark-universe-seeking-nancy-grace-roman-space-telescope-launches/)).
The telescope is named for Nancy Grace Roman, NASA's first chief
astronomer, whose advocacy in the 1960s helped build the case for putting
telescopes in space at all and who is often credited as a driving force
behind Hubble itself.

Roman still has work ahead before it opens its eyes on the universe.
Engineers will spend the coming months cooling and calibrating its
instruments as the observatory settles into its L2 orbit, with first
science images expected in early 2027. When they arrive, they're expected
to cover more sky in a single shot than Hubble managed in its first
several years combined — a wide-angle lens trained on the darkest,
least-understood parts of the universe.
