At 10:12 p.m. CEST on Friday, a two-stage rocket called Spectrum lifted off
from a pad at Andøya Space, on a Norwegian island well inside the Arctic
Circle, and did something no vehicle launched from continental Europe had
ever done: it crossed the 100-kilometer Kármán line, reached orbital
velocity, and released its payloads into low Earth orbit
([Isar Aerospace](https://isaraerospace.com/press/history-for-european-spaceflight-isar-aerospace-reaches-orbit-and-deploys-payloads-on-second-flight);
[ESA](https://www.esa.int/Enabling_Support/Space_Transportation/Boost/Isar_Aerospace_achieves_first_launch_to_orbit_from_continental_Europe)).
It was only the rocket's second flight.

The payloads themselves were modest and telling: a handful of educational
and startup satellites chosen through the German Space Agency's
Microlauncher Competition, the kind of small, low-stakes cargo that a
launch industry sends up not because the mission is glamorous but because
someone, somewhere, needs to prove the ride works. For most of the Space
Age, Europe hasn't had that ride at home. Ariane and Vega rockets fly from
Kourou, in French Guiana — a French overseas territory, but a long way
from Europe itself, geographically and logistically. A European company
wanting to put something small into orbit quickly has had few options that
didn't involve shipping hardware across an ocean first.

Isar Aerospace, a Munich-based startup founded in 2018, built Spectrum
specifically to close that gap. The company's first attempt, in March
2025, ended in an explosion less than 30 seconds after liftoff — an
outcome unremarkable by the standards of a young rocket program, where
early failures are closer to the rule than the exception. This second
flight went the distance instead, and did it with a vehicle that Isar
says was built inside a new 40,000-square-meter production facility
designed to turn out roughly 40 rockets a year. Five more Spectrum
vehicles are reportedly already in production.

"Today, Isar Aerospace opened space from continental Europe," CEO Daniel
Metzler said after the flight. "Launch continues to be the largest
bottleneck for the global space industry" — a bottleneck that a
still-small company in Bavaria has just made a little less severe for an
entire continent.

It's worth being precise about what changed and what didn't. This was one
successful flight of a small-lift rocket, not a mature launch industry.
Spectrum's payload capacity is a fraction of what an Ariane 6 or a Falcon
9 can carry, and one clean flight doesn't erase the memory of the first.
But orbital access has always started this way — a small vehicle, a light
payload, a pad somewhere unglamorous — before it becomes routine. Norway's
Arctic coastline joins French Guiana, Cape Canaveral, and Baikonur on the
short list of places from which the ground has fallen away into orbit,
and the list, for the first time from European soil, now includes
somewhere you could drive to.
