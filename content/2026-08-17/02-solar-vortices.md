---
title: Astronomers may have finally found what's cooking the Sun's atmosphere
kicker: Science
author: The Editors
summary: The world's largest solar telescope caught tiny whirlpools rippling across the Sun's surface for the first time, offering a leading answer to why the corona runs a hundred times hotter than the star beneath it.
order: 2
---

Why is the Sun's outer atmosphere, the corona, more than a hundred times
hotter than its visible surface? It is one of solar physics' oldest open
questions — heat is supposed to fade as it moves away from its source, not
intensify — and a team using the NSF Daniel K. Inouye Solar Telescope on
Maui now has a strong lead. Scientists there have captured the first direct
images of Kelvin-Helmholtz instabilities on the Sun's photosphere: small,
swirling vortices, spaced roughly 50 to 65 kilometers apart, rippling across
structures just tens of kilometers wide
([NSO](https://nso.edu/press-release/nsf-inouye-solar-telescope-enables-major-discovery-of-a-hidden-solar-process/);
[Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10871-3)).

The instability itself is a familiar one — it is what makes clouds ripple
and ocean waves curl when two fluid layers slide past each other at
different speeds. Seeing it on the Sun required resolving structures the
Inouye telescope, the largest solar instrument in the world, was only
recently sharp enough to catch. Researchers from the National Solar
Observatory, the NSF NCAR High Altitude Observatory, and the Max Planck
Institute for Solar System Research believe these vortices tangle and braid
the Sun's magnetic field, and that the energy released as those braids snap
and reconnect may be a significant piece of what keeps the corona so
fiercely hot.

"We believe that the discovery of Kelvin-Helmholtz instability in the solar
photosphere is a major step forward in our understanding of the dynamics
and evolution of solar and stellar plasma," said David Boboltz, deputy
director of the National Solar Observatory. Chief Technologist Thomas
Rimmele was more direct about the stakes: the mechanism, he said, "is likely
... part of the solution of the longstanding enigma of why stars have a
million degrees Kelvin hot corona." Researcher Friedrich Wöger cautioned
that the team is "only at the beginning of recognizing" how far the finding
reaches, which is itself the appealing part — a fifty-year mystery, and a
brand-new tool just sharp enough to start answering it.
