title: NASA's newest telescope just switched on its planet-hunting eye

summary: Three days after launch, the Roman Space Telescope powered up the Coronagraph Instrument that will block starlight to reveal worlds around other stars.

The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope launched on August 30 and is still a
month from its parking spot a million miles from Earth, but its team wasted
no time putting the hardware through its paces. On September 1, NASA
confirmed that Roman's Coronagraph Instrument had powered on successfully,
the first major activation of the mission's most technically ambitious
component ([NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/roman/)).

A coronagraph works by physically masking the light of a host star so that
the much fainter light reflected by an orbiting planet can be seen at all —
akin to blocking a car's headlights to spot a firefly hovering nearby. It's
a capability no NASA flagship mission has flown at this level of precision
before, and Roman's version is meant to prove out technology for
future missions that could image Earth-like planets directly.

The instrument now enters an extended calibration and testing phase that
will run for several months before it begins science operations in earnest.
There's no shortage of patience required: Roman's overall commissioning
period stretches roughly three months, as the observatory settles into its
gravitationally stable vantage point at the second Lagrange point and its
instruments — this coronagraph among them — are individually verified
against the vacuum and cold of deep space.

It's an early, unglamorous step in a mission built for exactly this kind of
incrementalism: survey speeds roughly a thousand times faster than Hubble,
aimed at dark matter, dark energy, and now, if the coronagraph performs as
hoped, direct pictures of the planets and dusty disks circling nearby stars.
