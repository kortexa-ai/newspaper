At 9:12 p.m. Eastern on Saturday, a Falcon 9 lifted off from Cape Canaveral
carrying eight Globalstar satellites bound for low Earth orbit. Thirty-eight
minutes and change later, at 6:50 p.m. Pacific — 9:50 p.m. back on the East
Coast — a second Falcon 9 left the pad at Vandenberg Space Force Base in
California carrying a classified U.S. Space Force payload called USSF-366.
Two rockets, two coasts, one company, well under forty minutes apart. It is
the shortest gap ever recorded between two orbital launches
([Space.com](https://www.space.com/space-exploration/launches-spacecraft/spacex-breaks-record-launch-doubleheader-ussf-366-globalstar-2r);
[Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/2026/08/15/live-coverage-spacex-to-launch-satellite-replenishment-mission-for-globalstar-from-cape-canaveral/)).

The previous record, also SpaceX's, stood at 65 minutes, set by a pair of
Starlink missions in August 2024. Cutting that gap nearly in half is less
about any single dramatic innovation than about a company that has spent a
decade turning orbital launch from an occasion into a schedule. Both boosters
flew back to Earth intact: B1090, making its 14th flight, touched down at
Landing Zone 40 near the Cape minutes after sending the Globalstar
satellites on their way, while B1088 landed on the droneship "Of Course I
Still Love You" in the Pacific — the 650th successful Falcon booster landing
to date. All eight Globalstar satellites deployed cleanly over a six-minute
span roughly 56 minutes after their launch.

There is a quieter kind of achievement buried in the choreography. Running
two independent launch campaigns on opposite coasts in parallel, with
separate pads, separate ground crews, separate weather windows, and separate
customers — one a commercial satellite operator replenishing an aging
constellation, the other the Space Force flying a payload it isn't
discussing — and having them converge to within 38 minutes takes the kind of
scheduling precision that used to be the exception in spaceflight, not a
Saturday night. The Globalstar mission itself had been delayed since May to
make room for exactly this kind of coordination.

The Globalstar flight carries its own small subplot. Globalstar has been
quietly rebuilding its satellite fleet as Amazon moves toward acquiring the
company, reportedly to extend direct-to-device connectivity for Amazon's Leo
broadband constellation — the kind of unglamorous infrastructure launch that
rarely makes headlines on its own, but that depends entirely on a launch
provider being able to fly reliably, and often. That reliability is the
real story here. Records like this one are not really about speed for its
own sake; they are a proxy for how routine and how safe repeated rocket
flight has become. A decade ago, landing one booster back on a droneship was
front-page news. On Saturday, SpaceX landed two, on two coasts, minutes
apart, and moved on to the next launch.
