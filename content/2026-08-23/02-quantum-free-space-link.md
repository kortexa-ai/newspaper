title: Entangled light just crossed 13 miles of open air on Long Island
kicker: Infrastructure
author: The Editors
summary: Brookhaven Lab and Stony Brook University beamed entangled photons through free air between two new towers, a step toward quantum networks that don't need buried fiber.
order: 2
---

Quantum networks have mostly been built along fiber-optic cable, which
works but is expensive to trench and limited to wherever the glass already
runs. This week, researchers at the U.S. Department of Energy's Brookhaven
National Laboratory and Stony Brook University showed a way around that
constraint: they sent entangled photons through 13 miles (21 kilometers) of
open air between two purpose-built towers, Stony Brook's "Quantum
Watchtower" and Brookhaven's "Quantum Lighthouse," and confirmed the
entanglement survived the trip
([Brookhaven National Laboratory](https://www.bnl.gov/newsroom/news.php?a=123096),
[Stony Brook University](https://news.stonybrook.edu/university/stony-brook-brookhaven-lab-researchers-demonstrate-wireless-capability-for-quantum-network/)).

The light left each tower through a fiber core about five microns wide,
crossed the gap as a free-space beam, and was recaptured on the other end.
Daytime runs checked pointing precision; nighttime runs, with less
background light to compete with, confirmed the photon pairs arrived still
linked by entanglement. The demonstration, marked with a ribbon-cutting at
the Lighthouse on August 21, extends New York's existing quantum network,
already the longest in the country with eight connected nodes, and adds a
node that needs no cable in the ground at all.

"This will provide a direct route to create entangled atomic systems across
long distances," said Eden Figueroa, director of Stony Brook's Quantum
Institute, who led the project alongside Brookhaven scientist Justine
Haupt. A free-space link like this one is cheaper and faster to deploy than
fiber over the same distance, and it opens the door to eventually linking
quantum computers, secure communication nodes, or sensing instruments
across a city or a campus without waiting for a trench crew. Yale
University is already building a facility to join the same network. It is
unglamorous infrastructure work, the kind that rarely makes headlines on
its own, but it is exactly the kind of plumbing that has to get built
before the more famous applications of quantum computing and quantum-secure
communication can run on top of it.
