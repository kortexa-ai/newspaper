The Sun's surface has been photographed for over a century, but it took the
sharpest solar telescope ever built to notice that parts of it swirl like
water going down a drain. Astronomers working with the NSF Daniel K. Inouye
Solar Telescope (DKIST) on Maui — the largest solar telescope in the world —
have captured plasma vortices on the solar surface small enough, and subtle
enough, that no previous instrument had resolved them
([Max-Planck-Gesellschaft](https://www.mpg.de/26900660/tiny-vortices-discovered-on-the-sun-s-surface)).

The team, a collaboration between the NSF National Solar Observatory, the Max
Planck Institute for Solar System Research (MPS) in Germany, and the High
Altitude Observatory, pointed DKIST's four-meter mirror at the edges of solar
granules — the convection cells, each 500 to 2,000 kilometers across, that
tile the Sun's visible surface like a bubbling pot. Observing in violet light
at 416 nanometers, where the telescope's resolution reaches its physical
limit of about 19 kilometers, they found rows of small, spinning structures
at the boundaries where granules meet concentrated magnetic fields. Some of
the vortices measured only about 20 kilometers across — a barely visible
fraction of a star 1.4 million kilometers in diameter — twisting and
breaking like waves curling over on a beach
([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-08-tiny-vortices-sun-surface.html)).

Matched against computer simulations that reproduced the same swirling
patterns in striking detail, the observations let the team confirm what was
causing them: Kelvin-Helmholtz instabilities, the same fluid-dynamics
phenomenon that curls clouds into wave shapes and stirs ocean currents,
occurring wherever two layers of plasma slide past each other at different
speeds. On the Sun, that shear appears to be a routine, ongoing process
happening across the surface, mixing magnetized and unmagnetized plasma and
possibly helping twist the Sun's magnetic field into the configurations that
eventually erupt as flares and coronal mass ejections. The findings were
published August 5 in Nature under the title "Ubiquitous Kelvin–Helmholtz
instabilities driving plasma mixing on the Sun"
([Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10871-3)).

"The newly discovered plasma vortices impressively demonstrate how minute
processes — at the limit of what we can resolve using all available
techniques — significantly determine the nature of our star," said Sami K.
Solanki, director of MPS and a co-author of the study.

It is a small thing to have found — literally, at 20 kilometers across it is
about as small as anything can be and still show up in a picture of the Sun.
But solar physicists have spent decades chasing the mechanisms behind two of
the field's stubborn open questions: how the Sun's outer atmosphere gets
millions of degrees hotter than its surface, and how its magnetic field
stores and releases the energy behind space weather. A newly confirmed,
everywhere-at-once process for mixing plasma and twisting magnetic field
lines is exactly the kind of missing piece those questions have been
waiting for. It took building the world's sharpest solar telescope to see
it — proof that sometimes the answer was there all along, just too small to
catch.
