---
title: Mercury's crust holds less silicon than anyone thought, and that points to a fierier past
kicker: Space
author: The Editors
summary: A cleverly calibrated infrared technique shows Mercury's surface is up to a quarter poorer in silicon dioxide than believed, evidence that its ancient lava welled up from deep, extensively melted mantle.
order: 2
---

Mercury has been visited, orbited, and mapped, but some of the most basic facts about what it's actually made of have remained stubbornly uncertain — largely because the instruments available for reading a planet's surface chemistry from a distance are blunter than anyone would like. A new study led by Christian Renggli at the Max Planck Institute for Solar System Research, working with colleagues at the Universities of Münster and Göttingen, has sharpened that picture considerably, and the answer is stranger than expected: Mercury's crust contains only about 37 percent silicon dioxide by mass — as much as a quarter less than earlier estimates ([Max Planck Institute for Solar System Research](https://www.mps.mpg.de/mercury-s-crust-formed-by-extreme-volcanism); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/09/260911003854.htm)).

The team got there with a nicely low-tech trick in service of a high-tech problem. Ground- and space-based telescopes read a planet's mineral makeup from the infrared light its surface emits, but translating that light into precise chemistry requires a calibration standard. So the researchers made their own: a series of tiny glass beads, each about half a millimeter across, with carefully controlled silicon dioxide content, standing in for volcanic rock of known composition. They tested the method against lunar samples, where the ground truth is already well established from Apollo-era rock, and then applied it to infrared observations of Mercury, including measurements from the Bok Telescope in Arizona.

The lower-than-expected silicon dioxide reading matters because it's a clue to how Mercury's volcanic rock was made in the first place. Silicon dioxide content tends to fall as source rock melts more completely and from deeper in a planet's mantle. A crust this poor in silica points to lava that originated from unusually hot, unusually deep, and unusually thorough melting — a more violent volcanic adolescence for the solar system's smallest planet than the standard picture assumed. "Our findings suggest that the volcanic rocks on Mercury formed from more deeply melted mantle material than previously assumed," Renggli said.

The finding won't stay untested for long. ESA and JAXA's BepiColombo mission, whose two probes separated from their shared transport module on September 3 in preparation for orbital insertion around Mercury this November, carries the MERTIS infrared spectrometer — an instrument built to measure exactly this kind of surface chemistry, at far higher resolution than any Earth-based telescope can manage. Renggli's team designed their calibration method with that rendezvous explicitly in mind: "Our study lays the groundwork for deriving the most accurate information possible about the silicon dioxide content of Mercury's surface from BepiColombo's measurements," he said. In a few months, a spacecraft already en route will get to check this ground-based estimate against the real thing — about as clean a test as planetary science gets.
