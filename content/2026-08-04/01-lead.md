Small modular reactors have spent the last decade as a slide-deck
technology: promising on paper, perpetually a few years from a working
plant. Valar Atomics is trying to change that math, and on August 3 it
raised the money to try at scale — a $1 billion Series B led by
Sequoia Capital, with partner Shaun Maguire joining the board, plus a
separate $200 million credit facility from Erebor and J.P. Morgan
([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/03/sequoias-shaun-maguire-leads-1b-round-for-nuclear-startup-valar-atomics/);
[SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2026/08/03/raising-1b-funding-valar-atomics-plans-mass-produce-small-nuclear-reactors-ai-industry/)).
The round values the Hawthorne, California company at roughly $6
billion, triple the valuation it carried after a $450 million raise
just four months earlier.

What's buying that confidence is a reactor that already works. In
June, Valar's Ward 250 achieved self-sustaining criticality — the
company says it's the first privately developed reactor to do so
outside a national laboratory, reaching that milestone about seven
months after its NOVA core first went cold-critical at Los Alamos. The
reactor is waterless, sidestepping the enormous water demands that
have complicated conventional nuclear plants, and Valar has already
paired one with an Nvidia Blackwell system as part of a 30-megawatt
"AI factory" project built with Nvidia — reportedly the first time an
advanced reactor has directly powered AI computing infrastructure.

The company's pitch has always been about factory economics rather
than reactor physics. Nuclear power's cost problem is less about the
fuel than about how plants get built: bespoke, site-specific,
multi-year construction projects that blow through budgets almost as
a matter of course. Valar's answer is to treat reactors like
manufactured products rather than civil-engineering projects — build
them the same way each time, in a factory, and let the unit economics
improve the way they do in any other manufacturing line. CEO Isaiah
Taylor has described the plan as a "tick rate" that shrinks with every
reactor built, moving from single units toward fleets of tens, then
hundreds, then eventually thousands produced annually
([Valar Atomics](https://www.valaratomics.com/docs/Announcing-our-1B-Series-B-Led-By-Sequoia)).

That AI data centers are the first customer in line is no accident.
Power-hungry GPU clusters have become the most visible bottleneck in
the industry's expansion, and utilities in several states are already
warning that grid capacity, not chips, may be the binding constraint
on the next few years of AI buildout. A factory-made reactor that can
be sited near a data center and skip a decade-long interconnection
queue is a specific answer to a specific, urgent problem — one that
also happens to be carbon-free.

None of this makes small modular reactors a solved problem. Valar has
demonstrated one reactor design working, not a supply chain producing
them at the volumes its roadmap describes, and the regulatory path for
licensing reactors built on an assembly line rather than one at a time
remains largely unwritten. But the company has now cleared the
milestone that has stalled most of its predecessors — a reactor that
actually runs — and has the capital, for now, to find out whether it
can build the second one faster than the first.
