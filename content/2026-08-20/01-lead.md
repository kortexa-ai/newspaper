At 7:35 a.m. local time on August 19, a methane-fueled rocket lifted off from
a launch pad in the Gobi Desert, sent its upper stage on toward orbit, and
then did something no Chinese company had done before: it brought its own
first stage back down to Earth, upright, on a set of legs. Eight minutes
after liftoff, LandSpace's Zhuque-3 booster settled onto a landing pad at the
Dongfeng Commercial Space Innovation Pilot Zone, making the Beijing-based
startup the first commercial Chinese company to land an orbital-class rocket
booster
([Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/2026/08/19/landspace-becomes-first-commercial-chinese-company-to-land-an-orbital-class-booster/);
[Space.com](https://www.space.com/space-exploration/launches-spacecraft/touchdown-private-chinese-rocket-aces-landing-on-2nd-ever-flight)).

It was only the second flight of Zhuque-3. The first, last December, reached
orbit successfully but lost its booster to an explosion during the landing
burn — the kind of setback that has dogged reusable-rocket programs
everywhere, including SpaceX's own years of exploded prototypes before
Falcon 9 first stuck a landing. LandSpace fixed whatever went wrong and came
back to nail it on the very next try. Zhuque-3 runs on liquid methane and
liquid oxygen rather than Falcon 9's kerosene, a propellant combination that
burns cleaner, leaves less residue on engine parts, and is generally
considered easier to reuse quickly — assuming a company can get the engines
to work reliably in the first place, which LandSpace has now shown it can
([SpaceNews](https://spacenews.com/zhuque-3-reaches-orbit-on-test-flight-first-stage-lost-during-landing-attempt/)).

This was also only the second time any Chinese rocket has landed on
domestic soil at all. The first came just over a month earlier, on July 10,
when a state-owned Long March 10B — built by the government's China
Aerospace Science and Technology Corporation — pulled off its own recovery.
That LandSpace, a private company founded in 2015, matched a state-backed
giant's feat within weeks is the real headline: it suggests reusability in
China is becoming a capability spread across a competitive field of
companies, not a trick held by one national champion.

Reusable boosters matter because they attack the single biggest cost in
spaceflight — building a rocket you only fly once. SpaceX's ability to fly
and refly Falcon 9 boosters is the main reason launch prices have fallen by
an order of magnitude over the past decade, turning constellations of
thousands of satellites, frequent resupply missions, and ambitious deep-space
programs from expensive rarities into routine business. A second country
now has more than one company that can plausibly chase that same economics,
which should mean more launch capacity, more competition, and — eventually —
cheaper access to orbit for everyone building something up there, not just
for China.

LandSpace hasn't yet re-flown a recovered booster, and turning "we can land
it" into "we can land it, refurbish it in days, and fly it again" is its own
multi-year engineering slog, one SpaceX is still refining after a decade of
practice. But sticking the landing twice, on only the second attempt, with a
propellant combination the company is betting its future on, is a strong
signal that LandSpace intends to be in that business for the long haul —
and that the group of companies capable of doing what once only SpaceX could
do just got a little bigger.
