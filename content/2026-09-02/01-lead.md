Christopher Hills cannot walk, cannot use his hands, and speaks through a
computerized voice assistant. He also flies drones — banking them over the
Sunshine Coast's beaches and hinterland, framing shots the way any
cinematographer would, using only the small, precise movements of his
head. This week the University of the Sunshine Coast (UniSC), where Hills,
30, is a design student, published the story of the app that makes it
possible
([UniSC](https://www.unisc.edu.au/about/unisc-news/news-archive/2026/september/chriss-drone-app-aims-to-open-the-skies-to-aspiring-filmmakers-with-disability)).

Hills was born with quadriplegic cerebral palsy. Years before he built
anything, he earned cinematography and film production qualifications at
RMIT — a filmmaker's training aimed at a body that could not hold a
camera. The gap between that ambition and his hands is what he spent the
last six years closing, working with a software developer to build a
mobile application that translates head movements and switch-based
controls, mounted on the headrest of his wheelchair, into a drone's flight
path. The system leans on tools that were not built for this purpose but
turn out to fit it well: Apple's accessibility features, the Xbox Adaptive
Controller, and consumer DJI drones, wired together into something new.
"I have been able to use Apple devices, the Xbox Adaptive Controller and
DJI drones to design an app that allows me to pilot a drone using only
head movements," Hills said.

He has already field-tested the system extensively, capturing aerial
footage across the Sunshine Coast's coastline that would not look out of
place in a travel documentary. The next steps are more bureaucratic than
technical: Hills is now pursuing approval from Australia's Civil Aviation
Safety Authority so the app can be used to fly commercially, and he has
opened a beta program inviting other pilots to test it. Dr. Leah Barclay,
a senior lecturer in design at UniSC who has watched the project develop,
said the innovation "will have such a positive impact on many people,"
and is already exploring how to fold it into university research on
assistive technology for older adults — a reminder that a tool built for
one specific body often turns out to serve many.

Hills is careful to describe the app as a starting point rather than a
finished product. "With a few design changes, the app will be useful for
people with a wide range of needs," he said, and he talks about the
switches and head-tracking not as a workaround but as a new kind of
instrument — one that happens to fly. His own ambitions are unambiguous:
"I would love to be part of producing a full-length feature film one
day," he said. "The next drone shot that you see in a film might be a
shot by me."

It is a small story, in the sense that one filmmaker's app will not
change an industry by itself. But it is the kind of small that Daybreak
exists to notice: a problem — a body that a hobby and a career were not
designed around — met not with accommodation but with invention, and
shared freely enough that it might now help someone else fly, too.
